## 1. Foundational Mindfulness (§11.1)

### Jon Kabat-Zinn (MBSR)
- **Universal:** Observation language always ("notice," "feel," "become aware"), never command language ("make," "force," "push"). Non-striving — the goal is not to achieve a state but to notice what's already happening. Mind-wandering is normal, not failure; gentle return without judgment.
- **Phase B pacing:** Verbal guidance every 10–15 seconds for beginners, widening to longer pauses as they settle. Voice unhurried but warm, not monotone.
- **Always applies:** "Wherever you go, there you are" — the child doesn't need to create a special state. They're already breathing, already feeling. Our scripts help them notice, not manufacture.

### Eckhart Tolle (The Power of Now)
- **Universal:** Observer activation — "you are not your thoughts, you are the one who notices them." One conscious breath can shift state. Presence is found in the gap between thoughts, not through effort.
- **Always applies:** The moment of noticing IS the skill, not what happens after. Our scripts should mark the noticing moment ("Right there — you just felt that") as the win, not the outcome.

### Michael Singer (The Untethered Soul)
- **Universal:** "You are the one who hears your thoughts" — consciousness is the witness, not the content. Thoughts and feelings are objects of awareness, not identity.
- **Always applies:** Reinforces defusion across all modules — children are learning they are NOT their worries, fears, anger, or restlessness. They are the one noticing those things.

### Thich Nhat Hanh
- **Universal:** Simplest possible instruction. Few words, long silences. Joy and ease, never effort or struggle. "Breathing is a joy." Voice should be "calm but not too soft — convey stability." Everything is an invitation to enjoy, not a task to complete.
- **Phase B pacing:** The fewest words that still guide. If silence can hold the moment, let it.
- **Always applies:** Our scripts should feel like a gift being offered, not a lesson being taught. The child should feel they are doing something pleasant, not performing correctly.

### Pema Chödrön
- **Universal:** Discomfort is not a sign of failure — it's the growth edge. "Lean into the sharp points." Resistance is information, not obstruction. The practice is to stay present WITH difficulty, not escape it.
- **Always applies:** If a child feels restless, bored, or uncomfortable during Phase B, this is not failure. Our rescue design (sustain, not repeat) already handles this — Chödrön validates the approach.

### Jack Kornfield
- **Universal:** Loving awareness — not detached clinical observation but warm, kind attention. The observer is not cold; the observer loves what it sees. Forgiveness and self-kindness are not add-ons; they pervade all mindfulness.
- **Always applies:** Our script voice should carry warmth. Even in Focus or Grounding modules, the tone is affectionate — we're helping children befriend their own experience, not analyze it.

### Tara Brach (RAIN)
- **Universal:** Recognize → Allow → Investigate → Nurture. Radical acceptance — "this too belongs." Whatever arises during practice is welcome; nothing needs to be fixed or changed.
- **Always applies:** Our scripts never imply a feeling is wrong or needs to be replaced. Calm replaces anxiety not because anxiety was bad, but because calm was always available underneath. Warmth toward whatever arises.

## 2. Neuroscience & Clinical Research (§11.2)

### Stephen Porges (Polyvagal Theory)
- **Universal:** Neuroception of safety MUST precede practice — a child in threat mode cannot engage the ventral vagal complex. Extended exhale is the key autonomic lever (not inhale). 80% of vagus fibers are afferent (body→brain), meaning bottom-up regulation works: change the body, change the brain state.
- **Always applies:** Phase A's warm creature-helping context is not decorative — it's clinically necessary to establish safety before interoceptive work. All Phase B scripts benefit from extended exhale emphasis where breath is involved.
- **Caution (universal):** No commanding tone. Safety cues must already be established. Trauma-exposed children may find interoceptive awareness activating — our scripts should be invitational, never forcing attention inward.

### Bessel van der Kolk (The Body Keeps the Score)
- **Universal:** Stress is stored in the body. The body needs to learn it is safe — this can't be done through talking alone. Bottom-up approaches (body→brain) are often more effective than top-down (thinking→feeling) for regulation. Observational language for body work ("notice your breath") not directive ("breathe into your belly").
- **Always applies:** Every Phase B script is, at its deepest level, teaching the child's body that it is safe. This is why the body-focused scripts (Grounding domain) exist, but the principle operates in ALL modules — even Thought Clouds works because the child's body settles when the mind stops fighting thoughts.
- **Caution (universal):** Attending to internal bodily sensations can surface trauma. Never force eyes closed. Start with externally-focused practices before internally-focused. Safety context is prerequisite.

### Peter Levine (Somatic Experiencing)
- **Universal:** Pendulation — the body naturally oscillates between activation and settling. Don't rush settling; let the body find its own rhythm. The body knows how to complete a stress cycle if given permission. "Felt sense" — vague, body-level awareness that precedes clear emotion.
- **Always applies:** Our scripts should respect the body's own timeline. If we guide "notice how you feel," the answer might be "weird" or "nothing" — both are valid. Pendulation means calm might come in waves, not all at once. Our scripts shouldn't imply a linear trajectory toward calm.

### Herbert Benson (Relaxation Response)
- **Universal:** Two conditions trigger measurable autonomic shift: (1) focused attention on a single thing, and (2) passive disregard of distracting thoughts. Simple, repeatable, no special conditions needed. Works in minutes.
- **Always applies:** Every Phase B script is structurally a relaxation response: focus on one anchor (breath, body, sound, etc.) + let go of distractions. Benson validates that this works even in very short durations — our 45–90 second window is sufficient for measurable autonomic change.

### Johannes Heinrich Schultz (Autogenic Training)
*NEW — added v1.1. Primary source for M17 (Sleep Induction / body heaviness module).*

- **Core technique:** Autogenic Training (AT), published 1932, is a passive self-induction method in which the practitioner focuses on verbal formulas that suggest bodily sensations — specifically **heaviness** in the limbs and torso, and **warmth** in the extremities. These sensations, when passively attended to rather than forced, reliably trigger the parasympathetic relaxation response and are associated specifically with sleep-onset physiology (N1 stage). AT is clinically validated for insomnia treatment across multiple meta-analyses (Stetter & Kupper review; Lichstein sleep treatment literature).
- **Heaviness formula:** The foundational AT exercise: "My right arm is heavy." The trainee does not try to make the arm heavy — they passively notice and invite the sensation. The heaviness spreads progressively through the extremities. Within a week of regular practice, a brief concentration on heaviness can shift the entire body toward parasympathetic dominance.
- **Mechanistic distinction from Jacobson (PMR):** Jacobson's PMR is active — the practitioner creates tension in order to release it. Schultz's AT is entirely passive — no muscular effort, only receptive attention to sensations already present or gently invited. This is a critical clinical distinction: PMR teaches via contrast (tension vs. release); AT teaches via surrender (noticing the body's natural heaviness without doing anything). The sleep-onset application strongly favors AT because active effort is counterproductive to N1 sleep transition.
- **Mechanistic basis for sleep:** The body's resting muscle tone decreases naturally as the nervous system shifts toward sleep. AT accelerates this by directing passive attention to the sensation of this decrease — the body feels heavier because it IS becoming heavier as muscles release tone. Attending to this sensation without efforting creates a positive feedback loop: noticing heaviness → deeper parasympathetic response → more heaviness → drift toward sleep.
- **Child adaptation:** The full AT protocol (six formulas: heaviness, warmth, heartbeat regulation, breathing, abdominal warmth, forehead cooling) is adult-designed. For ages 7–10: **use the heaviness formula only**, optionally followed by warmth. Verbal guidance is external (from Pip) rather than internal self-repetition. The instruction is invitational: "see if you can feel your arms getting heavier... not trying to make them heavy, just noticing." This preserves the passive concentration mechanism while removing the cognitive load of internal self-talk. Clinical support for adapted versions: the core mechanism (passive concentration on heaviness) is preserved; only the delivery method is simplified.
- **Evidence tier:** Strong for adult insomnia (multiple comparative RCTs, meta-analyses). Emerging for pediatric adaptation specifically — no dedicated pediatric AT RCT identified, but developmental feasibility is strong (children respond readily to body-sensation focus at this age) and adult mechanisms are neurophysiologically continuous.
- **Caution:** AT, like all interoceptive techniques, can surface distress in trauma-exposed children. The heaviness sensation should always be framed as pleasant and safe ("like sinking into the softest bed"). If a child finds the heaviness sensation uncomfortable, this should be honored — redirect to breath or sound. Never force.
- **Always applies:** In sleep/rest modules specifically, AT's core principle — that passive attention to the body's natural sleep-onset physiology accelerates sleep transition — is the primary mechanism. The child is not creating heaviness. They are noticing and welcoming what the body already does when it lets go.

**Key clinical reference:** Stetter F, Kupper S. "Autogenic training: a meta-analysis of clinical outcome studies." Applied Psychophysiology and Biofeedback, 2002. Found AT efficacious across stress-related disorders including insomnia. Schultz JH. Das Autogene Training, 1932 (foundational text). Luthe W. Autogenic Therapy (multi-volume), 1969 (English-language introduction).

### O'Hara, R. / Stanford School of Medicine — Pediatric Sleep Evidence
*NEW — added v1.1. Supporting evidence for all sleep/rest modules (M13, M17, and future Bork domain modules).*

- **Study:** O'Hara et al. (2021). School-based mindfulness curriculum (Pure Power/PureEdge) tested with polysomnography (objective brain activity measurement) in children. Two-year prospective observational cohort, N=115 children ages 8–11.
- **Key finding:** Children who received the mindfulness curriculum gained an average of **74 minutes of additional sleep per night** vs. pre-intervention baseline. Children in the control group (matched for SES) lost 64 minutes per night over the same period. REM sleep increased by approximately 30 minutes in the intervention group.
- **Mechanism reported:** The study found strongest sleep improvements in children who used mindful breathing exercises outside of school — suggesting the mechanism is bottom-up nervous system regulation (parasympathetic shift via breath-based practice), not simply stress reduction via top-down cognition. The authors note that N1 sleep onset is characterized by reduced body temperature, reduced breathing rate, and increased relaxation — all of which are directly produced by the practices taught.
- **Relevance to MindfulNest:** This is the strongest available pediatric polysomnographic evidence that mindfulness-based practices produce objective sleep improvements in the 7–10 age range. It validates the therapeutic rationale for the entire Rest domain (Bork), and specifically supports the design of sleep-onset modules as clinically meaningful, not merely soothing.
- **Citation:** O'Hara R et al. "A school-based health and mindfulness curriculum improves children's objectively measured sleep: a prospective observational cohort study." Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine, 2022. Published online 2021 at Stanford Medicine News.
- **Always applies:** Referenced in any Bork domain module dossier as supporting evidence for pediatric sleep efficacy of mindfulness-based interventions.

### Richard Davidson
- **Universal:** Neuroplasticity — mindfulness structurally changes the brain, and children show these changes. Improved attention, emotional regulation, and social cognition are measurable outcomes in children. Even brief practice matters.
- **Always applies:** Validates the entire project. Short, age-appropriate practices DO create real changes. We're not playing pretend — 45–90 seconds of genuine practice has neurological effects.

### Daniel Goleman
- **Universal:** Emotional intelligence has five components: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skill. Long-term meditation improves all five. Regular brief practice matters more than occasional long practice.
- **Always applies:** Our 12 modules collectively touch all five EI components. Individual scripts don't need to address all — each contributes to the whole. Frequency matters more than duration.

### Judson Brewer
- **Universal:** Curiosity is the antidote to craving/aversion habit loops. "Hmm, what does this feel like?" breaks the automatic reaction cycle. Mindful awareness is not about stopping habits but becoming curious about them.
- **Always applies:** Our Phase B scripts can use curiosity as a micro-tool: "I wonder what you'll notice..." is more effective than "try to feel calm." Curiosity keeps children engaged without performance pressure.

### Rick Hanson ("Taking in the Good")
- **Universal:** Positive neuroplasticity requires actively savoring good experiences for 15–30 seconds. The brain has a negativity bias; without deliberate savoring, positive states don't consolidate into lasting neural changes. "Let the good experience fill your body."
- **Always applies:** Directly validates our Win step design AND the end of Phase B. The last 10–15 seconds of any Phase B script should include a moment of noticing/savoring how the body feels. This isn't narrative decoration — it's the mechanism that turns a momentary state into a trait.

### Shauna Shapiro (IAA Framework)
- **Universal:** Every mindfulness practice has three components: Intention (why am I doing this?), Attention (what am I focusing on?), Attitude (how am I relating to what I notice?). Attitude should always be kind, curious, non-judgmental.
- **Always applies:** Useful diagnostic for script review. Every Phase B script should have a clear attention anchor, and the attitude conveyed by the script's voice should be kind and curious, never clinical or demanding. Intention is handled by the Phase A narrative context.

## 3. Self-Compassion (§11.3)

### Kristin Neff
- **Universal:** Three components of self-compassion: self-kindness (vs. self-judgment), common humanity (vs. isolation), mindfulness (vs. over-identification). Children benefit from self-kindness practice but may experience resistance — this is normal, not failure.
- **Always applies:** Our script voice models self-kindness. Even in non-Kindness modules, the tone should convey "you're doing great by simply being here." If a child can't do the practice, the voice should be warm, not corrective.

### Christopher Germer
- **Universal:** "Backdraft" — when people first direct compassion or kind attention toward themselves, it can initially surface pain or resistance. This is a normal phase, not a sign to stop. Proceed gently.
- **Always applies:** Any Phase B script that asks the child to notice themselves (as opposed to something external) may trigger mild discomfort. This is expected. Our rescue design handles the worst case, but scripts should be gentle in the "turn attention inward" moments.

## 4. Cognitive / Behavioral (§11.4)

### Steven Hayes (ACT)
- **Universal:** Cognitive defusion — thoughts are mental events, not facts. "I notice I'm having the thought that..." creates distance. Experiential avoidance (trying NOT to feel something) backfires. Acceptance + commitment to values-based action.
- **Always applies:** Our scripts never ask children to stop thinking, stop worrying, or push away feelings. The move is always toward noticing and accepting, never suppressing. This principle is universal but MOST critical in Focus and Courage modules.

### Williams, Segal, Teasdale (MBCT)
- **Universal:** "Decentering" — seeing thoughts as passing mental events rather than reflections of reality. Mindfulness prevents depressive relapse by changing the person's RELATIONSHIP to their thoughts, not the thought content.
- **Always applies:** Reinforces Hayes. Our scripts change the child's relationship to internal experience (thoughts, worries, body sensations) — they don't try to change the experience itself.

### Adrian Wells (MCT / ATT)
- **Universal:** Attention is trainable. Three attention skills: selective attention (focus on one thing), attention switching (move focus deliberately), and sustained attention (hold focus over time). Attention Training Technique uses competing stimuli to build flexibility.
- **Always applies:** Every Phase B script trains at least one of these three attention skills. Useful diagnostic: which attention skill is this script primarily training?

## 5. Child-Specific (§11.5)

### Daniel Siegel & Tina Payne Bryson
- **Universal:** "Name it to tame it" — putting words to feelings reduces amygdala activation. "Upstairs/downstairs brain" — regulation happens when upstairs brain (prefrontal cortex) connects with downstairs brain (limbic system). Integration, not control.
- **Always applies:** When our scripts ask children to notice what they feel, the noticing itself IS therapeutic — it connects upstairs and downstairs brain. This is the mechanism behind many of our modules, not just Worry Box.

### Susan Kaiser Greenland (Inner Kids)
- **Universal:** New ABCs: Attention, Balance, Compassion. Meditation with children should be PLAYFUL — not solemn or precious. "Play-Practice-Share-Apply" sequence. Ages 7–10: 3–10 minute practices. Primary goal is attention training, not relaxation (relaxation is side effect). Never punitive. Model authenticity.
- **Always applies:** Our Phase B scripts should never feel solemn or heavy. The tone is warm and playful even during quiet moments. A 7-year-old should feel like this is something fun and special, not medicine. Kaiser Greenland validates our entire gamification approach — play IS practice.

### Amy Saltzman (Still Quiet Place)
- **Universal:** "Still Quiet Place" — the pause between breaths, the space between thoughts. For children: practices must be warm, slow, frequent verbal guidance for under-8s. "Kindness and curiosity" as the two required attitudes. 2–5 minute practices for ages 7–10. Offer never force. Honor the child's experience.
- **Phase B pacing:** For youngest end of our range (age 7): more verbal guidance, shorter silences, warmer/slower voice. For age 10: can extend silences, more trust in their capacity.
- **Always applies:** "Offer never force" is our universal design principle. If a child doesn't want to close their eyes, that's OK. If they don't feel calm, that's OK. The practice is the offering; the outcome belongs to the child.

### Stuart Shanker (Self-Reg)
- **Universal:** REGULATION, not control. Self-regulation means recognizing and managing energy/arousal states — NOT suppressing feelings or forcing calm. "Reframe the behavior" — what looks like misbehavior is often a child overwhelmed by stress. Five domains of stress (biological, emotional, cognitive, social, prosocial).
- **Always applies:** CRITICAL GUARDRAIL. Our scripts teach regulation, never suppression. Thought Clouds is defusion, not thought-stopping. Belly Breathing is noticing calm arriving, not forcing calm. Worry Box is containing worries, not eliminating them. Brave Steps is approaching fear, not conquering it. If a script implies "push that feeling away" — it violates Shanker.

### Goldie Hawn / MindUP
- **Universal:** Evidence-based school curriculum demonstrating improved executive function and emotional regulation in children through brief, daily mindfulness practices. Structured three-part lessons (learn about the brain → practice → reflect) work for children.
- **Always applies:** Validates our pedagogical structure: Phase A (learn about the skill through creature narrative) → Phase B (practice) → Rescue/Win (reflect/celebrate). Our structure mirrors what works in classroom settings.

### Ross Greene
- **Universal:** "Kids do well if they can" — not "if they want to." When a child struggles, it's a skill deficit, not a will deficit. No guilt, no pressure, no punishment. Collaborative problem-solving, not imposed solutions.
- **Always applies:** FOUNDATIONAL. If a child can't do the practice, our response is never "try harder" — it's "that's OK, just being here is enough." Our scripts must convey zero performance pressure. This is why our Phase B includes a mind-wandering normalization beat and why our rescue is sustain-not-repeat.

### Generation Mindful
- **Universal:** Age-appropriate framing: "mindful ears" not "sustained attention practice." Invitational language: "I invite you to..." not "you must." Body-awareness language calibrated for 7–10 year olds. Practice when calm, use when stressed. Nighttime routines are natural entry points. 2–5 minutes for ages 7–10.
- **Always applies:** Our vocabulary filter. If a Phase B script uses a word or concept a 7-year-old wouldn't naturally understand, Generation Mindful's age calibration is the reference point. "Notice your belly" passes. "Observe your diaphragmatic engagement" does not.
- **Caution (universal):** For PreK-12, avoid holding or retaining breath, especially with trauma-exposed children. Holding breath can be a natural response to fear/distress and can be retraumatizing.

## Universal Phase B Script Principles (Cross-Source Synthesis)

These principles emerge from multiple sources and apply to EVERY Phase B script:

### Voice & Tone
1. **Warm, not clinical** — Kornfield, Thich Nhat Hanh, Kaiser Greenland, Saltzman
2. **Invitational, not commanding** — Generation Mindful, Saltzman, Porges, van der Kolk
3. **Observation language** — Kabat-Zinn ("notice," "feel"), never force language ("make," "push")
4. **Curiosity as engagement** — Brewer, Shapiro, Saltzman
5. **Few words, well-placed** — Thich Nhat Hanh, Saltzman (more guidance for younger children)

### Structure
6. **Safety context precedes interoception** — Porges, van der Kolk (Phase A provides this)
7. **One attention anchor per script** — Benson, Wells
8. **Mind-wandering normalization** — Kabat-Zinn, Kaiser Greenland (gentle return, not failure)
9. **Savoring beat at end** — Hanson (15–30 seconds of noticing how you feel)
10. **45–90 seconds is sufficient** — Davidson (neurological change occurs), Benson (autonomic shift measurable in minutes)

### Guardrails
11. **Regulation, not suppression** — Shanker (CRITICAL — test every script against this)
12. **No performance pressure** — Greene, Kabat-Zinn non-striving, Saltzman offer-never-force
13. **No breath-holding** — Generation Mindful trauma consensus
14. **Never force eyes closed** — van der Kolk
15. **Discomfort is valid data, not failure** — Chödrön, Levine, Germer (backdraft)
16. **Thoughts/feelings are events, not identity** — Hayes, Williams/Segal/Teasdale, Singer, Tolle

### Sleep/Rest Module Specific (Bork domain)
17. **Passive attention only** — Schultz (AT): effort is the enemy of sleep onset. The instruction is to notice, not to do.
18. **Heaviness is a portal** — Schultz: passive concentration on the body's natural heaviness as muscle tone decreases is the physiological pathway into sleep onset. Naming it helps the child recognize and welcome it.
19. **Bottom-up sleep induction** — O'Hara/Stanford: objective polysomnographic evidence confirms mindfulness-based practices produce measurable sleep improvements in children ages 8–11. The mechanism is physiological (parasympathetic regulation), not just soothing.
20. **Do not try to sleep** — Benson, Schultz: effort to sleep is counterproductive. The practice is relaxing the body; sleep arrives as consequence. Scripts must never say "try to fall asleep."
