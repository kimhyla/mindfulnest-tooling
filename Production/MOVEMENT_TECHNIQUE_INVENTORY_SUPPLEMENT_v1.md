### BA-M4: Finger Breathing ("Starfish Breath")

**Clinical name:** Five-finger breathing / Traced breathing
**Kid-friendly working name:** Starfish Breath

**Description:** The child holds one hand up, fingers spread. With the index finger of the other hand, they slowly trace UP the outside of the thumb (inhale), then DOWN the inside of the thumb (exhale). Continue up and down each finger: inhale up, exhale down. Five fingers = five complete breaths. Then reverse direction back across the hand for five more. The settling portion: both hands rest in lap, eyes closed, noticing how the breath continues at the rhythm the fingers taught it.

**Sources:**
- Kaiser Greenland (Inner Kids — finger breathing is one of her most-cited children's practices; provides tactile anchoring for breath rhythm)
- Generation Mindful (five-finger breathing as a core calming strategy for ages 5–10; one of the most widely replicated children's mindfulness techniques globally)
- Saltzman (body-anchoring for children — tactile + breath co-regulation)
- Benson (repetitive motor activity as relaxation response trigger — the tracing IS the mental focusing device)

**Domain assignment:** Breath Awareness (Tessa). The finger tracing serves the breath — it gives the inhale and exhale a physical shape the child can follow with their finger. The movement (tracing) is the breath's training wheel.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike BA-M1 (arms up/down — large movement), this is MICRO-movement — fingertip precision. Unlike BA-M2 (forward fold — gravity + breath), this is purely tactile-breath linkage. The hand provides a built-in counting system (five fingers = five breaths) that gives the child concrete progress markers without abstract counting.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. This is perhaps THE most widely used children's mindfulness technique worldwide. Tested and validated across the 7–10 range extensively. Requires no space — can even be done under a desk. Fine motor tracing is well within capability for this age.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (tracing up and down 5 fingers, ~5 seconds per finger) takes ~50 seconds for one full hand. Settling: "Now let both hands rest… your breath learned the rhythm from your fingers… it keeps going all on its own…" (30 seconds). Total ~80 seconds. Perfect fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** This is arguably the single most clinically validated children's movement-breath technique in existence. Its omission from the Base Inventory was a significant oversight. Multiple primary child-specific sources. Universal feasibility.

### BA-M5: Cat-Cow Spinal Breath ("Dragon Breathing")

**Clinical name:** Spinal flexion-extension with breath synchronization (Marjaryasana-Bitilakasana)
**Kid-friendly working name:** Dragon Breathing

**Description:** The child gets on hands and knees. On the inhale, they dip their belly toward the floor, lift their chin up, and arch their back (cow pose — "happy dragon looking up"). On the exhale, they round their back toward the ceiling, tuck their chin, and pull belly in (cat pose — "sleepy dragon curling up"). Alternate for 5–6 breath cycles. The movement is entirely driven by the breath. Settling: child sits back on heels or stands, eyes closed, noticing the warm, loosened feeling in their spine.

**Sources:**
- Kabat-Zinn (MBSR yoga — cat-cow is a foundational MBSR movement; the spinal flexion-extension is explicitly breath-synchronized in MBSR protocol)
- van der Kolk (yoga as therapy — spinal movement releases stored tension in the back; the spine is one of the primary sites of stress-held tension)
- Kaiser Greenland (adapted yoga for children — cat-cow is one of the "essential" poses for children's yoga; simple enough for beginners)
- Thich Nhat Hanh (movement as breath awareness — the spine's movement makes each breath visible and physical)

**Domain assignment:** Breath Awareness (Tessa). The breath controls the spine — inhale opens, exhale closes. The child SEES and FEELS their breath through their entire torso. This is the most whole-body breath-visibility technique in the inventory.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike BA-M1 (standing, arms), BA-M2 (standing, fold), and BA-M4 (seated, fingers), this is on hands and knees, using the SPINE as the breath's instrument. Different body position, different muscle groups, different sensation profile (spinal instead of peripheral). The only floor-based Breath Awareness technique.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Children naturally enjoy animal poses. Hands-and-knees is comfortable and stable (no balance concern). The cat/cow shapes are simple and intuitive. Requires floor space but easily done on carpet or a towel.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (5–6 cat-cow cycles, ~8 seconds per cycle) takes ~40–48 seconds. Settling: sit back on heels or stand → "Feel your spine right now… it's warm and loose after all that moving… notice how your breath is slow and deep even though you're not trying…" (30–35 seconds). Total ~70–83 seconds. Good fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** One of the most clinically established breath-movement sequences. Multiple primary sources. Extremely age-appropriate. Clean domain fit. The floor-based position adds variety to a domain that was previously all-standing.

### BA-M6: Exhale-Powered Blow ("Candle Breath")

**Clinical name:** Extended exhale through pursed lips with visual target
**Kid-friendly working name:** Candle Breath

**Description:** The child holds up one finger (the "candle"). They inhale through the nose, then exhale sloooowly through pursed lips, aiming their breath at the finger — trying to make the "candle flame" flicker but NOT go out. The slow, controlled exhale is the practice. After 4–5 candle breaths, they hold their hand to their mouth and feel the warmth of their own breath on their palm. Settling: hand down, eyes closed, noticing the slow rhythm their body learned.

**Sources:**
- Generation Mindful ("smell the flower, blow out the candle" is one of their signature children's breath phrases; pursed-lip exhale for ages 5–10)
- Porges (extended exhale as vagal brake activation — pursed lips NATURALLY extend the exhale without counting)
- Saltzman (breath practices for children — visual/imaginary targets make exhale extension effortless)
- Kaiser Greenland (blow-based breath practices for children; the finger serves as the "candle" — no prop needed)

**Domain assignment:** Breath Awareness (Tessa). Exhale extension through the physical mechanism of pursed lips and aim. The "movement" is the oral motor action and the hand positioning — minimal but genuine physical engagement.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike M2 (4-7-8 counting to extend exhale — cognitive), this extends the exhale through a PHYSICAL MECHANISM (pursed lips + aim). No counting needed. The exhale naturally lengthens when the child is trying not to "blow out the candle." Different pathway to the same parasympathetic result.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. "Blow out a candle" is universally understood. The finger-as-candle removes the need for any prop. Extremely simple. Works anywhere.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (4–5 candle breaths + hand warmth check) takes ~35–40 seconds. Settling: 30–35 seconds. Total ~65–75 seconds. Good fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Well-sourced, age-perfect, clinically sound. The pursed-lip mechanism is a clever alternative pathway to exhale extension that doesn't require the cognitive load of counting.

### PM-M4: Orienting Response ("Owl Look")

**Clinical name:** Slow orienting / Environmental awareness through deliberate head turning
**Kid-friendly working name:** Owl Look

**Description:** The child stands or sits. VERY slowly, they turn their head to the right — as far as is comfortable — taking 8–10 seconds for the full turn. They notice everything they see as their gaze sweeps across the room: colors, shapes, objects, light. Then slowly turn back to center. Then slowly to the left. The practice is DELIBERATE LOOKING — the slowest, most intentional visual scan of the environment. Settling: eyes forward, then closed. Notice how the room feels after you really SAW it.

**Sources:**
- Levine (Somatic Experiencing — the orienting response is one of Levine's PRIMARY techniques; it completes the instinctive head-turning that gets interrupted during stress; "orienting to the environment" tells the nervous system "I am safe here, I can see around me, there is no threat")
- Porges (Polyvagal — the orienting response activates the ventral vagal social engagement system; the neck muscles involved in head-turning are innervated by cranial nerves that connect to the vagal safety circuit)
- van der Kolk (environmental awareness as grounding; the body needs to know where it IS before it can settle; trauma disrupts spatial orientation)
- Kaiser Greenland ("slow looking" games for children — "see how many things you can notice when you turn your head really, really slowly")

**Domain assignment:** Present-Moment Awareness (Luna). This is pure noticing — deliberate, slow attention to the present environment. The child practices seeing what's actually HERE, right now, in this room. The slow speed transforms automatic looking into conscious observation. That's metacognition: "I'm watching myself notice things."

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike PM-M1 (walking — kinesthetic attention), PM-M2 (freeze game — contrasting states), and PM-M3 (balance — internal micro-adjustments), this is VISUAL attention with HEAD MOVEMENT. The orienting response is unique in the clinical literature — it's specifically a Somatic Experiencing staple that no other technique replicates. The therapeutic mechanism (completing an interrupted survival response) is clinically distinct from general "paying attention."

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Turning your head slowly requires zero athletic ability. The "owl" framing is perfect — owls are known for their head-turning. The challenge is keeping it SLOW, which the narrative framing supports: "Owls turn their heads so slowly that mice never notice them looking."

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (slow right turn ~10 seconds + center ~3 seconds + slow left turn ~10 seconds + center ~3 seconds = ~26 seconds; optionally repeat). Settling: "Now face forward, close your eyes… you just showed your body that you're safe here, that you can see everything around you… notice how that feels…" (30–35 seconds). Total ~60–65 seconds. Fits easily, even with two full cycles.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Levine's orienting response is clinically distinctive — no other modality uses it. Porges validates the mechanism. The "owl" branding is a gift for Luna's domain. This was a significant omission from the Base Inventory.

### PM-M5: Mindful Hand Exploration ("Discovering Hands")

**Clinical name:** Micro-movement awareness / Slow hand observation
**Kid-friendly working name:** Discovering Hands

**Description:** The child holds both hands in front of them. VERY slowly, they open and close their fingers — watching each finger move independently. Then they flip hands over, examining palms, then backs. They make a fist as slowly as possible, noticing every muscle. Then open as slowly as possible. The practice is microscopic attention to movement most people do unconsciously thousands of times per day. Settling: hands rest in lap, eyes closed, noticing the subtle aliveness — tingling, warmth, pulse — in hands that the child usually ignores.

**Sources:**
- Kabat-Zinn (MBSR — hand awareness as a foundational body awareness exercise; "feel the aliveness in your hands" is one of his most quoted instructions; micro-movement observation as mindfulness training)
- Tolle (The Power of Now — "Can you feel the energy in your hands?" as a direct present-moment anchor; the hands as the most accessible entry point to body awareness)
- Kaiser Greenland (slow-motion games for children; "do it as slowly as you possibly can" as attention training)

**Domain assignment:** Present-Moment Awareness (Luna). The practice is pure attention — noticing movements you've done ten thousand times but never actually WATCHED. The metacognitive insight: "My hands do incredibly complicated things and I never noticed before." The slowness transforms the automatic into the observed.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike PM-M1 (walking), PM-M2 (freeze/unfreeze), PM-M3 (balance), and PM-M4 (head-turning/visual), this focuses on the HANDS — micro-scale movement awareness. The hands are the most dexterous, most neurologically represented body part, making them the richest possible observation target.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Every child has hands. No space, balance, or coordination requirements. The "discovering" frame works: "What if you've never really LOOKED at your own hands before?" Children are fascinated by their own bodies when given permission to study them.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (30–40 seconds of slow hand exploration). Settling: 30 seconds. Total ~60–70 seconds. Good fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Kabat-Zinn and Tolle both use hand awareness as a primary entry point. Clean domain fit. Extremely accessible. High novelty factor for children.

### CO-M4: Carrying and Setting Down ("Heavy Feelings")

**Clinical name:** Embodied defusion through physical weight metaphor
**Kid-friendly working name:** Heavy Feelings

**Description:** The child picks up something heavy-ish (a thick book, a pillow, a backpack — anything available). They hold it out in front of them with both hands, arms extended. They hold it for 10–15 seconds while the Guide Bird talks: "That's what worry feels like when you carry it around all day. Heavy, isn't it? Your arms are getting tired." Then the cue: "Now set it down." The child sets the object down and lets their arms hang. The relief is immediate and physical. Settling: standing with empty arms, eyes closed, noticing the lightness.

**Sources:**
- Hayes (ACT — defusion through metaphor; "thoughts are things you carry, not things you ARE"; the physical act of putting something down embodies the ACT concept of willingness — you can choose to set it down)
- Chödrön (letting go of the storyline — the physical release of a heavy object IS the experience of "letting go" that Chödrön describes conceptually)
- Levine (completing a physical action — the body learning "I can put it down" through motor completion)
- Siegel & Bryson (physical metaphors for emotional concepts help children ages 7–10 understand abstract ideas; the weight metaphor is immediately felt, not just understood)

**Domain assignment:** Courage (Benson). Setting something down sounds simple, but for anxious children, "putting down" a worry takes COURAGE. The worry feels essential — "if I stop worrying, something bad will happen." This technique teaches through the body: you CAN set it down, your arms DON'T have to carry it forever, and the relief is real and immediate. That's approach-behavior toward the fear of letting go.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike CO-M1 (power pose — holding strength), CO-M2 (shaking — discharging energy), and CO-M3 (wall push — engaging with resistance), this is about RELEASE — specifically, choosing to release something you've been gripping. The weight metaphor is ACT's signature, making this the most cognitively rich courage-movement technique. It physically teaches defusion.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Holding something heavy is universal. The only constraint: the child needs an available object. A pillow, book, stuffed animal, or water bottle works. The Phase A demo can show the creature carrying something heavy and setting it down, making the object type flexible.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (picking up ~5 seconds + holding at arm's length ~15 seconds + setting down ~5 seconds) takes ~25–30 seconds. Could repeat once. Settling: "Notice your arms… they feel so light now… you set it down and you're FINE… your body learned something just now — you can carry hard feelings, and you can also put them down…" (30–40 seconds). Total ~60–75 seconds. Good fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Hayes's ACT metaphor literally embodied. Chödrön and Levine validate the mechanism. Perfectly age-appropriate. Clean Courage domain fit. The physical weight→release→relief sequence is clinically elegant.

### CO-M5: "Tug-of-War Drop" ("Letting Go of the Rope")

**Clinical name:** ACT metaphor embodied — cessation of struggle
**Kid-friendly working name:** Letting Go of the Rope

**Description:** The child mimes holding a rope in a tug of war (arms out, fists gripping, leaning back, pulling hard). They hold the struggle for 10–15 seconds — really pulling, really gripping. Then the cue: "What if you just… let go of the rope?" The child drops their arms. The "opponent" (the worry, the anger, the fight) is still there — but you're not wrestling with it anymore. Settling: standing relaxed, arms loose, eyes closed, noticing the relief of not fighting.

**Sources:**
- Hayes (ACT — the tug-of-war metaphor is one of ACT's MOST famous metaphors; "you're in a tug-of-war with a monster — the pit is between you — instead of pulling harder, drop the rope")
- Chödrön (stopping the fight — "nothing to do, nowhere to go, nothing to fix")
- Shanker (Self-Reg — regulation comes from STOPPING the fight with the feeling, not from winning it; "you can't regulate while you're still struggling")

**Domain assignment:** Courage (Benson). It takes courage to STOP FIGHTING. Children (and adults) believe that if they stop struggling against anxiety, it will overwhelm them. This technique teaches through the body that dropping the rope — stopping the fight — brings relief, not defeat. The courage is in the letting go.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike CO-M4 (carrying and setting down — a static weight), this is about ACTIVE STRUGGLE followed by cessation. The tug-of-war involves full-body tension, leaning, gripping — then sudden release. Different movement quality (struggle→stop vs. hold→release). Different ACT metaphor (tug-of-war vs. carrying). CO-M4 is about detachment from worry; CO-M5 is about stopping the fight with emotions.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Every child knows tug-of-war. The mime is intuitive. The metaphor is concrete and vivid. No prop needed — the rope is imaginary, which actually strengthens the metaphor ("the thing you're fighting with isn't even a real rope").

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (grip + pull ~15 seconds, drop ~instant, stand in relief ~5 seconds) takes ~20–25 seconds. Settling: "You dropped the rope… and the scary thing is still over there, but you're not fighting it anymore… and look — your arms feel better, your body feels better… you don't have to fight everything that scares you…" (30–40 seconds). Total ~50–65 seconds. Fits well.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** This is one of ACT's single most famous clinical metaphors, physically embodied. Clean Courage fit. Excellent for ages 7–10 (tug-of-war is universally understood). Distinct from all other courage techniques.

### KI-M4: Hugging Meditation with Stuffed Animal ("Heart Hug")

**Clinical name:** Conscious embrace / Three-breath hug
**Kid-friendly working name:** Heart Hug

**Description:** The child holds a stuffed animal (or pillow) against their chest and gives it a real hug — not a quick squeeze, but a sustained, gentle embrace. They take three slow breaths while hugging: first breath for themselves ("this hug is for me"), second breath for the creature they're hugging ("this hug is for you"), third breath for someone they care about who isn't here ("this hug travels to [person]"). Settling: child still holding the object gently, eyes closed, feeling warmth in their arms and chest.

**Sources:**
- Thich Nhat Hanh (hugging meditation — three conscious breaths while embracing; one of his signature practices; "the first breath is for presence, the second for happiness, the third for gratitude")
- Neff (self-compassion through physical touch — hugging activates the caregiving system; oxytocin release through sustained embrace)
- Porges (sustained chest pressure + warmth activates ventral vagal safety circuit)
- Kaiser Greenland (stuffed animal practices for children; "hugging your stuffy" as a named regulation practice; tactile comfort as emotional regulation tool)

**Domain assignment:** Kindness (Ember). The three-breath structure teaches compassion in three directions: inward (self-kindness), toward the object (care for another), and outward to someone absent (extending compassion beyond the room). This IS metta practice in physical form.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike KI-M1 (self-hug — no object, bilateral tapping), KI-M2 (rocking — vestibular, no object), and KI-M3 (warm hands on heart — hand-generated warmth), this uses an EXTERNAL OBJECT as the kindness target. The stuffed animal makes compassion TANGIBLE — the child is practicing care through the act of holding something gently. Also unique: the three-direction structure (self → object → absent person) mirrors classic metta meditation's expanding circles.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent with design note. Stuffed animals are universally available and loved by 7–10 year olds. A pillow works as alternative. The three-breath structure is simple. Concern: some 10-year-olds may feel "too old" for stuffed animals. Framing: "Even brave foxes keep something soft nearby" or framing the object as a comfort item, not a toy.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (picking up object + three slow hugging breaths ~25–30 seconds). Settling: "Keep holding gently… feel the warmth between you and your [stuffy]… that warm feeling is kindness… it goes both ways — from you to them, and from them right back to you…" (30–35 seconds). Total ~55–65 seconds. Good fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Thich Nhat Hanh's hugging meditation is a published, widely-practiced technique. Neff and Porges validate the mechanism. Clean Kindness fit. The three-direction structure adds therapeutic depth.

### KI-M5: Gentle Face Self-Touch ("Kind Face")

**Clinical name:** Facial soothing touch / Compassionate face holding
**Kid-friendly working name:** Kind Face

**Description:** The child places both hands gently on their own face — fingertips on forehead, palms on cheeks, or cupping their chin. They hold this gentle self-touch for 15–20 seconds, feeling the warmth of their own hands on their face. Then they slowly move hands down to hold their own jaw, then rest hands on their neck/collarbone. The touch is soft, slow, and deliberate — the way you'd touch someone you love who's upset.

**Sources:**
- Neff (Mindful Self-Compassion — "soothing touch" includes face as a target; the face is where we most instinctively touch when distressed — forehead when tired, cheeks when sad, chin when thinking; deliberate self-face-touch reclaims this as conscious self-care)
- Germer (MSC practices — gentle self-touch as embodied kindness; face-holding as specific variation)
- Porges (facial touch activates the trigeminal nerve → vagal safety circuit; the face is the primary site of the social engagement system; touching your own face mimics being comforted by a caregiver)
- van der Kolk (self-touch as body-based safety signal; the face-hands connection is among the most calming touch patterns)

**Domain assignment:** Kindness (Ember). Touching your own face the way a loving person would is an act of self-compassion. The face is intensely personal — we don't let strangers touch our faces. When a child holds their own face gently, they're practicing the deepest form of "I'm here for myself."

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike KI-M1 (shoulder self-hug — arms on shoulders), KI-M3 (warm hands on heart — chest/belly), and KI-M4 (hug with object), this targets the FACE — the most emotionally expressive and neurologically sensitive body part. The vagal mechanism is different (trigeminal nerve, not just ventral vagal).

**Age appropriateness:** Good with caveat. Some children may feel silly holding their own face. Framing: "Put your hands on your cheeks the way your mom does when she wants to look at you with love." The caregiver-mimic framing normalizes it. Simple, quiet, no space requirements.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (hands on face ~15 seconds, slow transition to jaw/neck ~10 seconds) takes ~25–30 seconds. Settling: hands rest in lap, eyes closed, "Your face remembers that kindness… the warmth stays even after your hands move away…" (30 seconds). Total ~55–60 seconds. Compact fit with generous settling.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Well-sourced through the Neff/Germer self-compassion literature and Porges vagal mechanism. The face-specificity is clinically distinctive. May need careful narrative framing but therapeutically sound.

### RE-M4: Child's Pose / Resting Fold ("Cozy Curl")

**Clinical name:** Balasana (Child's Pose) — restorative resting posture
**Kid-friendly working name:** Cozy Curl

**Description:** The child kneels on the floor, then folds forward until their forehead touches the ground (or as close as comfortable), arms extended forward or resting alongside the body. This IS the resting position — no active movement, just allowing gravity and the forward fold to create deep rest. Hold for 20–30 seconds. The body weight is fully supported by the floor. Settling: slowly sit up, then sit quietly with eyes closed, noticing how the body feels after being curled and held by the ground.

**Sources:**
- Kabat-Zinn (MBSR yoga — child's pose is used as a RESTING pose within MBSR; represents "complete surrender to gravity and the floor")
- van der Kolk (restorative yoga — forward folds with head-below-heart activate parasympathetic response; the fetal-adjacent position activates deep safety signals)
- Kaiser Greenland (child's pose as a named rest pose for children; "turtle shell" or "sleeping seed" variations; children find it naturally comforting)
- Levine (curling inward as a natural self-protective posture that, in a SAFE context, becomes self-soothing rather than defensive)

**Domain assignment:** Relaxation / Embodied Coping (Bork). This is PURE rest — the most surrendered, most physically released position possible while not lying down. No effort, no engagement, no attention training. Just: give your body to the floor and rest.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike RE-M1 (melting — gravity release, standing, dynamic), RE-M2 (stretching — active extension), and RE-M3 (squeeze-and-sigh — tension-release with vocalization), this is SUSTAINED STILLNESS in a restorative shape. The only movement technique where the "movement" is getting INTO a position and then STAYING. The resting IS the practice.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Children adopt this position instinctively when tired. It's called "child's pose" for a reason. Some children with knee issues may need a modification (pillow under knees or between calves and thighs). Requires floor space and a surface soft enough for knees (carpet, towel, or mat).

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (kneeling and folding forward ~8 seconds + holding ~20–25 seconds) takes ~30 seconds. Settling: slowly rising + seated stillness with eyes closed (30–35 seconds). Total ~60–65 seconds. Compact. Could extend the hold if more settling time is available.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Perhaps the most universally recognized resting yoga pose. Multiple primary sources. Instinctively comfortable for children. Clean Bork domain fit.

### RE-M5: Shoulder Shrug and Drop ("Mountain Shrug")

**Clinical name:** Trapezius engagement and release / Upper body tension discharge
**Kid-friendly working name:** Mountain Shrug

**Description:** The child lifts both shoulders up toward their ears — as HIGH as possible — and holds for 5 seconds. Then DROPS them completely. Repeat 3–4 times. Each cycle: lift (slow) → hold (5 seconds) → drop (sudden). The drop is the key — it should feel like the shoulders FALL rather than being lowered. Settling: standing or sitting with deliberately low, relaxed shoulders, eyes closed, noticing the released quality in the neck and upper back.

**Sources:**
- Kabat-Zinn (MBSR body awareness — shoulder tension as the most common stress-holding pattern; deliberate shrug-and-release as a mini-PMR for the upper body)
- Saltzman (body-based practices for children — shoulder shrugs as age-appropriate tension release; "lift your shoulders up like they want to touch your ears, then let them fall")
- Levine (tension-release through the upper body; the shoulders are where "carrying the weight" is literally felt)
- van der Kolk (upper body holds "bracing" tension — the startle response pattern; releasing shoulders releases the startle)

**Domain assignment:** Relaxation / Embodied Coping (Bork). This is micro-PMR targeted at the single most common tension site. The therapeutic intent is release, not awareness.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike RE-M3 (Big Sigh — whole body squeeze-release + vocalization), this isolates the SHOULDERS specifically. Unlike M11 (full PMR sequence — multiple body parts), this is just one body region, done repeatedly, with the emphasis on the DROP rather than the contrast. Simpler, more focused, more repeatable in daily life. This is the movement technique most likely to become a lifelong habit.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. The simplest movement technique in the entire inventory. Every child can shrug. No floor, no space, no props, no coordination.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (4 shrug-drop cycles × ~8 seconds each) takes ~32 seconds. Settling: 30 seconds. Total ~62 seconds. Very clean fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Multiple sources. The simplest possible movement technique. Most likely to transfer to daily use. Clean domain fit.

### RE-M6: Spinal Twist ("Wringing Out")

**Clinical name:** Seated spinal rotation / Detoxification metaphor twist
**Kid-friendly working name:** Wringing Out

**Description:** The child sits cross-legged on the floor. They place their right hand on their left knee and gently twist their torso to the left, looking over their left shoulder. Hold for 8–10 seconds, breathing into the twist. Return to center. Repeat on the other side. The twist creates a wringing sensation through the torso — like squeezing water out of a towel. Settling: facing forward, eyes closed, noticing the open, released feeling in the torso.

**Sources:**
- Kabat-Zinn (MBSR yoga — seated twist as a core MBSR movement; "the twist creates space where there was compression")
- van der Kolk (spinal rotation releases fascia-held tension in the torso; the twist accesses muscles that other movements miss — the obliques, intercostals, and deep rotators)
- Kaiser Greenland (adapted seated twists for children — simpler than adult versions, shorter holds)

**Domain assignment:** Relaxation / Embodied Coping (Bork). The twist and release serve relaxation — "wringing out" tension. The metaphor is powerful for children: "Imagine your body is like a wet towel and you're gently squeezing the tiredness out."

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike RE-M2 (stretching — linear extension), RE-M4 (child's pose — forward fold), and RE-M5 (shoulder shrugs — vertical release), this is ROTATIONAL. Twisting accesses muscles that no other movement direction reaches. Unique movement plane in the inventory.

**Age appropriateness:** Good. Seated twists are within capability for ages 7–10. "Don't force it — just go as far as feels good" is the key instruction. Some children may be tight through the torso — that's fine, the twist is gentle, not competitive.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (two twists, ~12 seconds each with transition) takes ~30 seconds. Settling: 30 seconds. Total ~60 seconds. Compact.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Well-sourced through MBSR yoga. Unique movement plane. Clean domain fit. The "wringing out" metaphor is clinically apt and kid-friendly.

### BI-M5: Jaw Clench and Notice ("Stone Jaw, Soft Jaw")

**Clinical name:** Masseter awareness / Jaw tension-release with interoceptive focus
**Kid-friendly working name:** Stone Jaw, Soft Jaw

**Description:** The child clenches their jaw tightly — teeth pressed together, jaw muscles hard — for 5 seconds. Then completely releases, letting the jaw hang slightly open, lips soft. Notice: "Where do you feel that? Temples? Ears? Neck?" Repeat 3 times. The jaw holds more unconscious tension than any other facial muscle group, and most children have never noticed it. Settling: jaw soft, eyes closed, noticing if any tension creeps back (it usually does — the jaw is stubborn).

**Sources:**
- van der Kolk (The Body Keeps the Score — jaw tension as primary stress indicator; the masseter is one of the strongest muscles in the body and one of the first to engage during stress)
- Levine (Somatic Experiencing — jaw as a key site where stress "gets stuck"; releasing the jaw releases the entire craniofacial tension pattern)
- Porges (jaw muscles are connected to cranial nerve circuits involved in social engagement; jaw tension inhibits ventral vagal tone; jaw release facilitates safety)
- Kabat-Zinn (body scan — jaw awareness as a specific check-in point; "soften the jaw" as a standard MBSR instruction)

**Domain assignment:** Body Awareness / Interoception (Bramble). The practice is NOTICING — discovering that your jaw holds tension you didn't know about. The question "where do you feel it?" is the interoceptive training. Most children are shocked to discover their jaw was clenched without their knowledge. That revelation — "my body was doing something I didn't even know about" — IS Bramble's territory.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike BI-M1 (feet — stomping), BI-M2 (whole body — sequential squeeze), BI-M3 (weight — standing), and BI-M4 (heartbeat — internal organ), this targets the FACE/JAW — a completely different body region with unique clinical significance. The jaw's connection to stress is stronger than any other single muscle group.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Children can easily clench and release their jaw. The discovery element ("I didn't know my jaw was tight!") is fascinating for this age. No space, coordination, or prop requirements.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (3 clench-release cycles × ~10 seconds each) takes ~30 seconds. Settling: 30 seconds. Total ~60 seconds. Efficient.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** van der Kolk and Levine both identify the jaw as a primary stress site. Porges validates the vagal mechanism. Clean Bramble domain fit. High novelty factor — most children have never deliberately noticed their jaw.

### BI-M6: Slow Fist Clench → Open Palm ("Holding and Releasing")

**Clinical name:** Hand awareness through deliberate grasp and release
**Kid-friendly working name:** Holding and Releasing

**Description:** The child holds both hands open, palms up. VERY slowly — over 5 seconds — they close their hands into fists, noticing every moment of the closing: fingers curling, knuckles tightening, nails pressing into palms. Hold the fist for 3 seconds. Then VERY slowly open, noticing every stage of release: fingers uncurling, palm spreading, fingertips separating. The slowness is the teaching — the child discovers dozens of micro-sensations in a movement they usually do in a fraction of a second. Repeat 2–3 times. Settling: hands resting open in lap, feeling the tingling in palms.

**Sources:**
- Kabat-Zinn (MBSR — hand awareness as a core mindfulness practice; "close your hand slowly and feel every moment of the closing")
- Levine (hands as expression of the grasp/release cycle — the body's fundamental binary between holding on and letting go; "the hands tell us what the body wants to do")
- Hayes (ACT — open hands as embodied acceptance; clenched fists as embodied avoidance; the physical posture affects the emotional state)
- Tolle (The Power of Now — "feel the energy in your hands" as a gateway to body awareness)

**Domain assignment:** Body Awareness / Interoception (Bramble). The practice is DETECTION — discovering sensation in a movement that's usually unconscious. The slowness amplifies signals that normally flash by unnoticed. The child builds interoceptive literacy: "I can feel tiny things in my body when I pay attention."

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike BI-M2 (Bear Hug Scan — squeezing body regions quickly for gross sensation), this uses EXTREME SLOWNESS to reveal SUBTLE sensation. Different speed, different scale, different teaching point. BI-M2 says "your body talks loud when you squeeze it." This says "your body talks QUIETLY all the time — slow down enough to hear it."

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Opening and closing hands requires zero ability. The challenge is SLOWNESS — 7-year-olds may speed up. The instruction must be specific: "Take five whole seconds to close your fist. That's one-mississippi, two-mississippi…"

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (3 slow clench-release cycles, ~15 seconds per cycle) takes ~45 seconds. Settling: 30 seconds. Total ~75 seconds. Good fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Multiple primary sources. Extreme simplicity. Clean domain fit. The slow-speed approach is clinically distinct from all other interoceptive techniques in the inventory.

### BI-M7: Face Scrunching / Social Engagement Muscles ("Funny Faces")

**Clinical name:** Facial muscle engagement and release / Social engagement system activation
**Kid-friendly working name:** Funny Faces

**Description:** The child makes deliberate facial expressions in sequence: (1) Scrunch everything tight — eyes squeezed, nose wrinkled, mouth pursed. Hold 5 seconds. Release. Notice. (2) Big surprise — eyes wide, eyebrows up, mouth open. Hold 5 seconds. Release. Notice. (3) Biggest smile possible — cheeks up, eyes crinkled. Hold 5 seconds. Release. Notice. After three expressions, the child sits with a neutral, soft face and notices what each part of their face feels like.

**Sources:**
- Porges (Polyvagal Theory — the facial muscles are the PRIMARY effectors of the social engagement system; the muscles around the eyes, mouth, and middle ear are innervated by cranial nerves V, VII, IX, X, and XI; engaging these muscles deliberately activates the ventral vagal safety circuit)
- van der Kolk (facial expression affects emotional state — bottom-up regulation through the face; facial muscle engagement reduces amygdala activation)
- Kaiser Greenland (making faces as a children's body awareness game; facial expression recognition and production as social-emotional learning)
- Siegel & Bryson ("name it to tame it" extended — noticing facial expressions helps children connect emotions to body signals; facial interoception as emotional literacy)

**Domain assignment:** Body Awareness / Interoception (Bramble). The child discovers that their face has MUSCLES they can feel and control — and that those muscles are connected to emotions. "When your face makes a happy shape, does your body feel happy? When it makes a scared shape, does your body feel anything?" This is interoceptive-emotional literacy through the face.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike BI-M5 (jaw only — one muscle group, tension focus), this engages the ENTIRE face through a sequence of deliberate expressions. The emotional-interoceptive connection ("does making this face change how you feel inside?") is clinically unique. No other technique explores the bidirectional face→emotion→body pathway.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Making funny faces is inherently delightful for ages 7–10. High engagement. The "surprise" and "smile" expressions are fun, not clinical. The framing matters: this is playful exploration, not forced emotion.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (3 expressions × ~10 seconds each) takes ~30 seconds. Settling: soft face, eyes closed, noticing facial sensations and any emotional echoes (30 seconds). Total ~60 seconds. Efficient.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Porges's social engagement system is the specific clinical foundation. The face is the body's most neurologically dense movement region. Clean Bramble domain fit. Extremely engaging for children.

### XD-3: Mountain Pose / Standing Meditation ("Tree Roots")

**Clinical name:** Tadasana — deliberate standing with postural awareness
**Kid-friendly working name:** Tree Roots

**Description:** The child stands completely still — feet hip-width, arms at sides, eyes open. ### XD-4: Siegel Hand Model Gesture ("Brain in My Hand")

**Clinical name:** Hand model of the brain / Embodied neuroscience metaphor
**Kid-friendly working name:** Brain in My Hand

**Description:** The child makes Siegel's hand model: thumb folded into palm (the "emotional brain/amygdala"), fingers wrapped over thumb (the "thinking brain/prefrontal cortex"). This is the integrated brain. Then they "flip the lid" — fingers spring open, thumb exposed. That's what happens when emotions overwhelm the thinking brain. Then they slowly, deliberately fold the fingers back down over the thumb — "putting the lid back on." The physical gesture teaches brain integration through movement.

**Sources:**
- Siegel & Bryson (this IS their signature technique — the "hand model of the brain" from The Whole-Brain Child and No-Drama Discipline; one of the most widely taught neuroscience-for-kids tools in the world)
- Kaiser Greenland (references and uses the hand model in children's mindfulness curricula)

**Domain options:** Could be Present-Moment Awareness (Luna — metacognition about how the mind works) or Courage (Benson — "when I flip my lid, I can put it back"). The metacognitive element is strong — the child learns to OBSERVE their own brain state through a physical model.

**Strength rating: BORDERLINE as a standalone Phase B movement module.** The hand model is clinically brilliant but may work better as a Phase A teaching tool or as an integrated moment within another module rather than a standalone 60–90 second practice. The physical movement (opening and closing one hand) may be too thin for a full Phase B movement module. Flag for Kim: standalone module or integrated teaching tool?

### XD-5: Savoring Pose / Taking In the Good ("Golden Moment")

**Clinical name:** Embodied positive neuroplasticity / Savoring with physical gesture
**Kid-friendly working name:** Golden Moment

**Description:** The child thinks of one good thing — something that made them smile today (or ever). They place both hands on their chest and take three slow breaths, deliberately HOLDING the good feeling in their body. The Guide Bird narrates: "Let that good feeling get bigger… feel it filling your chest, your arms, your whole body… you're not just remembering it, you're KEEPING it." The physical gesture (hands on chest + deliberate breaths) anchors the positive memory in the body.

**Sources:**
- Hanson (Taking in the Good — this is Hanson's PRIMARY contribution; the 15–30 seconds of deliberate savoring that creates positive neural changes; the body must FEEL the good experience, not just think about it)
- Neff (self-compassion includes receiving good things, not just managing hard things)
- Kornfield (loving awareness includes receiving beauty and goodness)
- Saltzman (gratitude practices for children — body-anchored appreciation)

**Domain options:** Kindness (Ember — receiving goodness is a form of self-kindness) or Relaxation (Bork — positive feelings promote relaxation). Leans Kindness because the core skill is RECEIVING — allowing yourself to be nourished by something good — which is a self-compassion practice.

**Strength rating: STRONG as Kindness.** Hanson's "taking in the good" is clinically validated and neurologically grounded. The physical gesture (hands on chest) adds the movement component. Could be a powerful Ember module.

## REJECTED CANDIDATES (With Reasoning)

**Sun Salutation sequence** — too many poses, too complex for a single module. Individual poses from the sequence ARE included (cat-cow, forward fold, mountain, child's pose).

**Full yoga flow / vinyasa** — violates one-pose-or-short-sequence rule. Modules teach ONE thing.

**EMDR eye movements** — too clinical, requires trained supervision, inappropriate for unsupervised app use.

**Martial arts stances** — insufficient source coverage in the 26 approved sources. van der Kolk mentions martial arts generally but provides no specific technique guidance for children.

**Dance / free movement** — too unstructured for the Phase B guided format. No specific approved source provides a guided free-movement protocol for children that fits our structure.

**Handstand / headstand / inversions** — safety concerns for unsupervised 7-year-olds. No approved source recommends inversions for children without direct supervision.

**Drumming / rhythmic percussion** — van der Kolk discusses drumming but it requires instruments/props not available in every setting. Could be reconsidered if a "body percussion" version (patting thighs, clapping) is developed — flag for future consideration.

**Legs-up-the-wall restorative pose** — requires a clear wall, sufficient floor space, and the ability to lie on the floor with legs elevated. Too many environmental constraints.

**Yoga Nidra body rotation** — no approved source covers yoga nidra specifically. Kabat-Zinn body scan covers similar territory without requiring a yoga nidra source. Moved to "Needs Source Validation" in Base Inventory.

**Tai Chi / Qigong movements** — Benson mentions tai chi as relaxation but no approved source provides child-adapted tai chi guidance. Moved to "Needs Source Validation" in Base Inventory.

**Pinwheel / Bubble Breathing** — requires a physical prop (pinwheel or bubble wand). Candle Breath (BA-M6) achieves the same exhale extension without a prop using the finger-as-candle method. The propped version could be a therapist-guided variant but not an app-delivered module.

**Body percussion / patting** — thin source coverage. Kaiser Greenland mentions patting activities but not as a standalone guided practice. Could be developed if a source is validated. Flag for future consideration.
