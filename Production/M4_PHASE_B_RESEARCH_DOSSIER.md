# Phase B Research Dossier: Module 4 — Mindful Listening

## Technique: Sustained auditory attention on a fading stimulus (singing bowl/bell to silence)
## Phase A Metaphor: "Follow the Sound Home" — child listens to a singing bowl, follows its resonance as it fades, resists tapping too early (patience IS the skill), and discovers a golden silence bloom at the end. The silence is warm and safe, not empty.
## Phase A Vocabulary: "jumping at every sound," "sharpen your Focus magic," "follow sounds all the way to silence," "quiet focus," "stay in that listening place," "those incredible ears of yours," "the quiet at the end is safe and even beautiful"

---

## Relevant Sources (from §11)

1. **Adrian Wells (MCT / ATT)** — PRIMARY. Attention Training Technique uses sustained attention on auditory stimuli as the core training mechanism. Wells identifies three trainable attention skills: selective attention, sustained attention, and attention switching. M4's bell-to-silence arc is a child-adapted sustained attention exercise.
2. **Herbert Benson (Relaxation Response)** — Focused attention on a single stimulus + passive disregard of distractions produces measurable autonomic shift. The bell-to-silence arc is structurally a compressed relaxation response: focus on one thing, let everything else go.
3. **Thich Nhat Hanh** — The "bell of mindfulness" tradition. Listening to a bell as an invitation to return to the present moment. The bell is not a test or command — it is an offering. Joy in the act of listening itself.
4. **Jon Kabat-Zinn (MBSR)** — Sound meditation as a formal MBSR practice. Present-moment awareness through sensory experience. The sound is an anchor, like the breath — something always available to return to. Non-striving: you don't "try" to hear; you simply listen.
5. **Susan Kaiser Greenland (Inner Kids)** — Attention as the PRIMARY skill for children ages 7–10, not relaxation (relaxation is a side effect). "Listening games" as play-based attention training. Children learn attention through engagement, not discipline.
6. **Amy Saltzman (Still Quiet Place)** — The "still quiet place" concept maps directly to M4's golden silence moment — the space at the end of the sound where everything settles. Child-specific pacing: warm, slow, frequent verbal guidance for younger children.
7. **Ross Greene** — "Kids do well if they can." If a child can't sustain attention through the fading sound, that's a skill gap, not defiance. Zero performance pressure. The practice is the invitation; what happens belongs to the child.

## Findings by Source

### 1. Adrian Wells (MCT / Attention Training Technique)

**Guided practice sequences:**
Wells' ATT protocol uses a structured attention exercise with three distinct phases: (1) selective attention — focus on a single sound while other sounds are present, (2) attention switching — deliberately move focus between sounds, (3) divided attention — try to hold multiple sounds simultaneously. For M4, the relevant phase is selective attention and sustained attention: hold focus on ONE stimulus (the bell) as it changes (fades). Wells' exercises typically use everyday sounds (traffic, voices, clock ticking) at varying distances. The therapeutic premise is that attention is a skill that can be trained, like a muscle.

**Child-specific language:**
Wells' published protocols are adult-oriented (designed for metacognitive therapy with anxiety/depression). No child-specific language exists in his source material. However, the PRINCIPLE translates directly: give the child one clear thing to focus on, make the focusing task inherently interesting (fading sound is naturally engaging), and frame the practice as skill-building ("training your magic ears") not performance ("try to concentrate harder").

**Physical cues:**
Wells does not prescribe specific body positions for ATT. The emphasis is on the auditory attention task itself, not on physical anchoring. For M4, this means: the body positioning instruction should be minimal — comfortable sitting, eyes closed — so the child's full attention can go to listening. Unlike breathing modules, there is no body-based feedback mechanism (no hand on belly). The ear IS the feedback mechanism.

**Common failure modes:**
- Giving up when the sound gets very quiet (the hardest part of sustained attention is maintaining focus as the stimulus weakens — this is exactly why M4's design uses a fading bell)
- Trying to "hold on" to the sound through effort/tension (striving defeats the purpose — listening requires receptivity, not effort)
- Getting bored or distracted during the silence portion (the gap between sound and silence is where children often lose focus — this is also where the therapeutic benefit occurs)
- Switching to internal chatter during silences ("Is it over? Did I miss it? When does the next sound come?")
- Confusing "paying attention" with "trying hard" — sustained attention is relaxed alertness, not effortful concentration

**Therapeutic mechanism:**
Wells' core insight: metacognitive beliefs about attention ("I can't control my thoughts," "worrying helps me prepare") maintain anxiety. ATT disrupts these beliefs by demonstrating that attention IS controllable — the patient learns they can choose where to direct focus. For children, this translates to: the experience of successfully following a sound to silence teaches the child "I can focus when I choose to." This is implicitly self-efficacy training. The fading stimulus is key: it gets HARDER to attend to, yet the child succeeds, proving their capacity.

**Pacing notes:**
Wells' ATT sessions are typically 10-12 minutes for adults. Our 60-120 second window requires compression. The principle to preserve: start with an easy attention task (sound is loud and clear), progressively increase difficulty (sound fades), then hold the most difficult state (silence). The difficulty gradient should feel natural, not forced.

**Cautions:**
- Wells warns against using ATT when a patient is in acute distress — the attention task should be practiced when relatively calm. This aligns with our "practice when calm, use when stressed" principle.
- ATT should not be framed as thought suppression or distraction — it's about training the attention system, not avoiding thoughts
- The exercise should never become a performance evaluation — "How well did I focus?" defeats the purpose

### 2. Herbert Benson (Relaxation Response)

**Guided practice sequences:**
Benson's relaxation response requires exactly two conditions: (1) a mental device — something to focus on (sound, word, image), and (2) passive attitude — when distractions arise, gently return to the focus without judgment. The bell-to-silence arc satisfies both: the bell IS the mental device, and the instruction to "just keep listening" IS the passive attitude. Benson demonstrated that this response occurs within minutes, even in novices. The response is physiological: decreased heart rate, lowered blood pressure, reduced cortical activation.

**Child-specific language:**
Benson's work is clinical/adult. However, his simplicity principle is directly child-applicable: "Pick one thing. Focus on it. When your mind wanders, come back." For M4: "Listen to the bell. When your mind goes somewhere else, come back to the bell." No complexity needed.

**Physical cues:**
Benson recommends a comfortable position, eyes closed, and muscle relaxation before beginning the attention exercise. For M4: settle the body first, THEN begin listening. The body settling is the container; the listening is the practice.

**Common failure modes:**
- Trying too hard to concentrate (the "passive" in "passive attention" is essential — efforting prevents the relaxation response)
- Self-monitoring ("Am I relaxed yet?") which creates a meta-cognitive loop that blocks the response
- Expecting immediate results — Benson notes the response develops over repeated practice sessions, not necessarily in the first attempt

**Therapeutic mechanism:**
Focused attention + passive disregard triggers a measurable autonomic shift: decreased sympathetic nervous system activity, increased parasympathetic tone. This occurs via the hypothalamic-pituitary pathway. For M4: the bell provides the "focused attention" component. As the bell fades, the child's sustained attention creates deeper autonomic settling. The silence at the end is where the relaxation response is most active — the child is still attending (to nothing), which paradoxically deepens the parasympathetic shift.

**Pacing notes:**
Benson's original protocol recommends 10-20 minutes, but his research shows measurable autonomic change in as little as 3-5 minutes. Our 60-120 second window is shorter than this, but with the bell's natural fading arc (which creates sustained attention without effort), even brief exposure to the attention-silence cycle may produce micro-doses of the response.

**Cautions:**
- Do not use as the sole intervention for clinical anxiety — Benson positions it as one tool among many
- Some people experience "relaxation-induced anxiety" — paradoxical distress when the body begins to relax (this is the Levine pendulation principle). In M4, the bell provides a focal anchor that prevents free-floating anxiety during silence.

### 3. Thich Nhat Hanh

**Guided practice sequences:**
Thich Nhat Hanh's bell meditation follows a beautiful sequence: (1) the bell is invited to sound (never "struck" — language of invitation, not force), (2) the listener stops whatever they are doing, (3) follow the sound with full attention, (4) enjoy the sound — "listening to the bell is a pleasure," (5) when the sound fades, notice the silence, (6) the silence is not empty — it is full of presence.

His "bell of mindfulness" practice in Plum Village communities uses a real bell struck at random intervals during the day. Everyone stops, breathes three times, and listens. The bell is "a friend, calling you back to the present moment."

**Child-specific language:**
Thich Nhat Hanh wrote extensively for children. Key language patterns:
- "Breathing in, I listen to the bell. Breathing out, I feel happy." (Linking listening to positive emotion)
- "The bell is your friend" (not a teacher, not a test, not an authority)
- "Just enjoy" (recurring — the practice is pleasant, not dutiful)
- "Listen until there is no more sound. Then listen to the silence."
- "The silence is as beautiful as the sound" (reframes silence from absence to presence)
- "When you hear the bell, you can go back to yourself"

**Physical cues:**
Minimal — stop what you're doing, sit quietly, breathe naturally while listening. No rigid posture. The emphasis is entirely on the quality of attention, not the position of the body. For children: "Sit however feels good. Close your eyes if you'd like."

**Common failure modes:**
Thich Nhat Hanh doesn't frame listening in terms of failure. His approach is entirely invitational — if the child doesn't listen, that's fine. The bell will ring again. However, the implicit challenge for children is: treating the bell as a test to "pass" rather than a gift to receive. M4's "tap too early" mechanic addresses this directly — the child learns patience through natural feedback, not correction.

**Therapeutic mechanism:**
Listening practice interrupts the "mental discourse" — the constant stream of internal narration. When fully attending to a sound, the thinking mind becomes quiet naturally (not through force). This is presence — the mind is where the ears are, not where the thoughts are. For children: the bell provides a concrete anchor that pulls them out of mind-wandering without requiring them to understand meditation theory.

**Pacing notes:**
Thich Nhat Hanh advocates for extremely sparse verbal guidance during listening. Let the bell speak. The guide's job is to ring the bell and then be silent. For M4's Phase B script: after the initial setup instruction, the narrator should become very sparse — let the sound (and then the silence) fill the space. This is one of the most silence-heavy scripts in the entire 12-module set.

**Cautions:**
- Never rush the silence — if the silence is cut short, the most therapeutic moment is lost
- The bell should never startle — it should arrive gently (M4's Phase B bell is preceded by the 3-second lead-in, which prevents startle)
- Do not narrate during the listening — the child needs uninterrupted auditory space

### 4. Jon Kabat-Zinn (MBSR)

**Guided practice sequences:**
Kabat-Zinn includes sound meditation as a formal MBSR practice. His sequence:
1. Begin with breath awareness to settle
2. Expand awareness to include sounds — "Let the sounds come to you" (don't reach for them)
3. Notice sounds without labeling or judging — just the raw sensory experience
4. When a sound fades, notice the silence that follows
5. When the mind wanders to thinking ABOUT sounds (labeling, reacting), return to the raw hearing
6. "Receiving sounds just as they are, without needing to identify or react to them"

**Child-specific language:**
Kabat-Zinn's sound meditation uses observation language: "notice," "receive," "let the sounds come to you." The critical instruction is RECEPTIVE, not ACTIVE — you don't "try to hear," you "let yourself hear." For M4: the child doesn't chase the sound; they open to it. This maps well to the Phase A vocabulary where the child "follows" the sound — following is receptive tracking, not aggressive pursuit.

**Physical cues:**
Same as his breath meditation: comfortable position, eyes closed, body settled first. For sound meditation specifically: he sometimes suggests opening the ears "as if they were satellite dishes, receiving whatever comes." This playful instruction could work well for ages 7-10.

**Common failure modes:**
- Labeling sounds ("that's a car," "that's a bird") instead of experiencing the raw sound
- Trying to control which sounds to hear vs. not hear
- Getting lost in thoughts triggered by sounds (a siren triggers worry, a bird triggers a memory) — the practice is to return to the sound itself
- For M4 specifically: analyzing the bell sound ("Is it getting quieter? Am I still hearing it?") instead of simply listening

**Therapeutic mechanism:**
Sound meditation trains "present-moment awareness through sensory experience" — the sound is always NOW. You can't hear the past or future. This makes auditory attention an inherently present-moment practice. For children: this is simpler than breath-awareness (breath has conceptual complexity — "Am I breathing right?") because sound just IS. You either hear it or you don't. No performance anxiety.

**Pacing notes:**
Kabat-Zinn suggests 20-30 minutes for formal sound meditation (adults), with periodic verbal guidance. For M4's compressed version: the bell provides natural pacing — as it fades, the practice deepens automatically. Verbal guidance should be minimal during the listening itself but present at transitions ("Now the sound is very soft... keep listening... now notice the silence...").

**Cautions:**
- Some participants find silence uncomfortable or anxiety-provoking — the "golden silence" in M4 should be brief enough to be therapeutic without triggering avoidance
- Sound meditation can increase hypervigilance in trauma-exposed individuals (listening for danger). M4 mitigates this by framing the listening as following ONE specific sound (the bell), not scanning the environment

### 5. Susan Kaiser Greenland (Inner Kids)

**Guided practice sequences:**
Kaiser Greenland uses listening exercises extensively in her curriculum for children:
- "Listening to the bell" — ring a bell, follow the sound, raise your hand when you can't hear it anymore (group practice)
- "Sound scavenger hunt" — close eyes, identify as many sounds as possible (attention expansion)
- "Focused listening" — choose one sound in the environment, stay with it for 30 seconds
She frames attention as the master skill: "If you can pay attention, you can learn anything."

**Child-specific language:**
- "Listen with your whole body" (not just ears)
- "See how far the sound can travel" (makes listening an adventure)
- "When the sound is gone, listen to what's left" (introduces silence as a positive presence)
- "Your ears are like superheroes — they can hear things other people miss" (empowerment)
- "Let's see if you can catch the very last tiny bit of the sound" (gamification of sustained attention — directly mirrors M4's "follow the sound home" concept)

**Physical cues:**
Kaiser Greenland often has children place a hand on their heart or belly during listening, to provide a physical anchor alongside the auditory one. For younger children (7-8), this dual anchor can prevent restlessness during the listening silence. For M4: consider whether the narrator suggests a light physical anchor ("place your hands in your lap, nice and still") before the listening begins.

**Common failure modes:**
- Competitive listening ("I heard it longer than you!") — in group settings. Not applicable to our solo app experience, but the PRINCIPLE applies: do not frame listening as a contest.
- Fidgeting during the silence portion (body restlessness undercuts auditory attention)
- Announcing the end of the sound too early (M4's Phase A already trains this through the "tap too early" mechanic — the child arrives at Phase B already understanding that patience is part of the skill)

**Therapeutic mechanism:**
Kaiser Greenland frames attention training as the FOUNDATION of all other mindfulness skills: "Children who can pay attention can then learn to regulate emotions, show compassion, and manage impulses." For M4: the listening practice isn't "just" a Focus exercise — it's building the attentional infrastructure that makes every other module possible.

**Pacing notes:**
For ages 7-10: Kaiser Greenland recommends 3-5 minutes of focused listening. Within that: bell rings, 20-30 seconds of listening to the fade, 5-10 seconds of silence, brief verbal check-in, then repeat. For M4's compressed format: one bell cycle with longer silence is more appropriate than multiple short cycles. The child gets ONE deep listening experience, not several shallow ones (aligns with One Demo Cycle rule from Phase A).

**Cautions:**
- Children with sensory processing differences may find sustained listening exercises overwhelming or frustrating — keep duration short, keep it invitational
- Never use listening exercises as punishment or "settling down" tools — they should always be framed as special, exciting skills

### 6. Amy Saltzman (Still Quiet Place)

**Guided practice sequences:**
Saltzman's "Still Quiet Place" concept is the space between: between breaths, between sounds, between thoughts. She guides children to discover these gaps as safe, pleasant resting places. Her listening exercise: "After you hear the bell, listen... listen... listen... and when the sound is completely gone, notice that still, quiet place inside you."

**Child-specific language:**
- "The still quiet place" — a felt sense, not a physical location
- "It's always there, waiting for you" (empowering — the child already has it)
- "You might feel it in your chest, or your belly, or just everywhere" (non-prescriptive)
- "Kindness and curiosity" — the two attitudes she teaches for all practices
- "I wonder what you'll notice..." (curiosity as entry point)

**Physical cues:**
Comfortable sitting or lying. Saltzman emphasizes that children should not be forced into rigid positions. For listening: "Sit however feels good, and let your ears do the work."

**Common failure modes:**
- Trying to FIND the "still quiet place" like hunting for a hidden object (it's not somewhere you go — it's what's there when you stop doing)
- Confusing stillness with freezing — the still quiet place is soft, not rigid

**Therapeutic mechanism:**
Saltzman's insight: the therapeutic moment is not during the sound — it's during the silence. The sound is the vehicle; the silence is the destination. For M4: the golden silence bloom in Phase A is the visual expression of this concept. In Phase B, the script should mark the silence moment as the peak therapeutic experience — "Right there. That quiet? That's your Focus magic."

**Pacing notes:**
For ages 7-8: more verbal guidance through the silence ("Keep listening to the quiet... stay right there in that still place..."). For ages 9-10: longer unguided silence is tolerable and beneficial. M4's script should aim for the middle — brief verbal anchoring during silence, but not so much that it fills the space.

**Cautions:**
- "Honor the child's experience" — if the child says "I didn't feel anything," that's valid. Don't correct or promise they will next time.

### 7. Ross Greene

**Guided practice sequences:**
Greene does not offer guided meditation scripts. His contribution is philosophical: the frame through which we design ALL practice experiences.

**Child-specific language:**
"Kids do well if they can" — if a child can't sustain attention on the fading bell, the problem is not effort or willingness. It may be developmental (7-year-old attention span), environmental (noisy setting), or emotional (too activated to settle). The script should never imply the child needs to "try harder."

**Common failure modes:**
Greene would reframe all "failure modes" as lagging skills, not character flaws. A child who "fails" at listening hasn't failed — they've shown us where their current attention capacity is. The practice is meeting them there.

**Therapeutic mechanism:**
By removing all performance pressure, Greene's philosophy creates the conditions for genuine skill development. A child who feels safe to "fail" at listening will try again tomorrow. A child who feels judged will avoid the practice.

**Cautions:**
- Never frame the listening exercise as something the child "should" be able to do
- Never compare to other children or to the child's own past performance
- If the Rescue fires (child didn't sustain attention), the sustain action ("keep that quiet focus...") must feel like encouragement, not correction

## Cross-Source Synthesis

### Points of Agreement

All seven sources agree on these principles for M4:

1. **The sound is the anchor, not the goal.** The therapeutic value is in the sustained attention process, not in "hearing correctly." (Wells, Benson, Kabat-Zinn, Thich Nhat Hanh)

2. **Silence is therapeutic, not empty.** The absence of sound is where the deepest autonomic shift occurs. (Saltzman, Thich Nhat Hanh, Benson, Kabat-Zinn)

3. **Receptive, not effortful.** Listening is LETTING sound come to you, not reaching for it. "Passive attention" (Benson), "just enjoy" (Thich Nhat Hanh), "let the sounds come to you" (Kabat-Zinn). The child opens; they don't grab.

4. **Minimal narration during listening.** The narrator should become very sparse once the bell begins. The sound and silence need space. (Thich Nhat Hanh, Kabat-Zinn, Saltzman)

5. **Zero performance pressure.** Whether the child follows the sound to completion or not, they practiced. (Greene, Kaiser Greenland, Kabat-Zinn)

6. **One sustained listening arc, not multiple short ones.** For our compressed duration, one deep bell-to-silence experience is more valuable than several rushed ones. (Kaiser Greenland pacing, One Demo Cycle rule)

### Points of Tension

1. **Physical anchor vs. pure listening:** Kaiser Greenland suggests a physical anchor (hand on heart) alongside auditory attention. Wells and Thich Nhat Hanh favor pure auditory focus with no physical component. **Resolution:** For M4, use minimal physical setup ("sit comfortably") but do not add a hand-on-heart or hand-on-belly anchor. This is a Focus module — the ear IS the instrument. Adding a body anchor dilutes the auditory attention training. Exception: if a Grounding/Rest module ever uses listening, the body anchor would be appropriate there.

2. **Narration during silence:** Saltzman suggests verbal anchoring during silence for younger children ("stay right there in that still place"). Thich Nhat Hanh suggests pure silence. **Resolution:** Use very brief, sparse verbal anchoring during the first silence exposure — one soft sentence — then let the silence hold. M4 is the child's first focused listening experience; total silence may feel abandoned rather than therapeutic. But keep narration to an absolute minimum (see Thich Nhat Hanh's principle: if silence can hold the moment, let it).

3. **Sound labeling vs. raw experience:** Kabat-Zinn warns against labeling sounds ("that's a bell"). But M4's design inherently labels it — the child knows it's a bell from Phase A. **Resolution:** Not a problem. Kabat-Zinn's warning is about scattered environmental sound meditation where labeling creates mental chatter. M4 uses a single, known, intentional sound. Knowing it's a bell doesn't create labeling distraction — the child is following ONE sound, not cataloging many.

### Phase A Alignment Check

**Phase A established:**
- "Follow the Sound Home" metaphor — following a sound as it fades
- "Tap too early" mechanic — patience training, the sound isn't done yet
- "Golden silence bloom" — the silence at the end is warm, golden, safe
- Luna (owl) "jumping at every sound" — the problem is reactive, unfocused hearing
- The skill is sustained, patient, focused listening
- Key vocabulary: "follow," "all the way to silence," "quiet focus," "listening place"

**Phase B alignment check:**
- ✅ The clinical literature strongly supports sustained attention on a fading stimulus as effective attention training (Wells, Benson)
- ✅ The "silence as destination" concept aligns perfectly with Saltzman's "still quiet place" and Thich Nhat Hanh's bell tradition
- ✅ The "patience IS the skill" framing (from the tap-too-early mechanic) aligns with Benson's "passive attitude" and Kabat-Zinn's non-striving
- ✅ No Phase A vocabulary conflicts with clinical guidance
- ✅ The Focus domain framing (attention training, not relaxation) aligns with Wells and Kaiser Greenland
- ⚠️ One consideration: Phase A uses a visual metaphor (golden bloom). Phase B is eyes-closed. The script must bridge from visual to felt sense — "That golden quiet — feel it right now" — without requiring the child to visualize. The warmth should be described as a body sensation, not a visual.

### Recommended Approach

Based on all sources, the M4 Phase B script should:

1. **Open with minimal physical settling** (3-5 seconds). Body comfortable, eyes closed. No hand-on-belly — this is an auditory module.

2. **Bridge from Phase A** using the "Follow the Sound Home" vocabulary. "You followed that sound all the way to the quiet place. Now it's your turn to do it for real."

3. **This is a Focus domain cognitive module** — it needs the PREVIEW section (8-section structure per spec). Before the bell rings, tell the child EXACTLY what will happen: "In a moment, you'll hear a sound. Your job is to follow it — all the way, even when it gets so quiet you can barely hear it. And when it's completely gone? Stay in that silence. That's where the magic is."

4. **Ring the bell.** Then the narrator becomes extremely sparse. The bell speaks.

5. **Minimal guidance during fading** — perhaps one soft sentence when the bell has faded significantly: "It's getting quieter... stay with it..."

6. **Mark the silence moment** — this is the therapeutic peak. Brief verbal anchoring: "There it is. The quiet. Stay right there." Then actual silence for 3-5 seconds.

7. **The DEEPENING phase** is a second bell cycle with even LESS narration — the child is trusted more. The silence extends slightly longer.

8. **Landing** names the felt sense: "That feeling — that quiet, steady focus — that's your magic." Do NOT evaluate performance.

9. **Exit** trails off gently into the Rescue: "Stay right there. Just keep listening..."

### Elements to Incorporate

(Specific elements from sources that should appear in the script, with attribution)

1. **"Let the sound come to you"** — Kabat-Zinn's receptive listening instruction. Don't reach for it; open to it.
2. **"The silence is as beautiful as the sound"** — Thich Nhat Hanh's reframe. Silence is a presence, not an absence.
3. **Brief verbal anchoring in silence** — Saltzman's approach for younger children. One sentence, then let silence hold.
4. **The bell as friend, not test** — Thich Nhat Hanh. The bell is inviting you to listen, not testing whether you can.
5. **Curiosity framing** — Brewer (via Quick-Ref). "I wonder how far you can follow this sound..." engages without pressuring.
6. **Savoring the silence** — Hanson (via Quick-Ref). The last 10-15 seconds should include noticing how the quiet feels. This consolidates the experience into lasting neural change.
7. **"Your ears are incredible"** — Kaiser Greenland empowerment framing. Matches Phase A's "those incredible ears of yours."

### Elements to Avoid

(Patterns warned against by sources, with attribution)

1. **"Try to concentrate"** — Wells: sustained attention is relaxed alertness, not effortful concentration. Never use "try" or "concentrate."
2. **"Block out other sounds"** — Kabat-Zinn: don't fight distractions; let them pass. If the child hears other sounds, that's fine — return gently to the bell.
3. **Filling the silence with narration** — Thich Nhat Hanh: the silence IS the practice. Don't talk through it.
4. **"Good job listening"** — evaluative language mid-meditation violates Forbidden Patterns. No evaluation until Rescue/Win.
5. **Extended silence without any anchor** — for ages 7-8, more than 5-6 seconds of total silence with no verbal touchpoint may feel abandoned rather than meditative (Saltzman, Kaiser Greenland).
6. **"Can you hear it?"** — turns listening into a test with a right/wrong answer. The child may panic if they can't hear the bell anymore. Instead: "Stay with whatever you hear... even if it's just the quiet."
7. **Breath instruction** — this is NOT a breathing module. Do not introduce "breathe in... breathe out..." The attention anchor is the sound, not the breath (Wells, Benson both emphasize single-anchor focus).
8. **Visualization instruction** — do not ask the child to "see" the golden bloom with eyes closed. Transition the visual to a felt sense: "feel that warmth" or "notice how quiet it is inside."
