## Findings by Source

### 1. Levine (Somatic Experiencing)

**Guided practice sequences:**
Levine's somatic empowerment: (a) Notice the body's defensive posture (contracted, small, protected). (b) Invite the body to complete the OPPOSITE movement — the one it would make if it felt safe and powerful. (c) Stand tall. Feet grounded. Arms open or raised. Chest lifted. (d) Hold the expanded posture and NOTICE what changes in felt experience. (e) Track the shift: what happens in the chest, belly, shoulders as the body takes up space? Levine frames this as "completing the active defense" — the body's incomplete self-protective response finds resolution through expansion rather than contraction.

**Child-specific language:**
Levine's language adapted for children: "Make yourself as BIG as you can." "Stand like the tallest, strongest thing you can imagine." "Open your arms like you're trying to hold the whole sky." Simple, physical, concrete.

**Physical cues:**
Feet shoulder-width apart (stable base). Arms open wide or raised overhead. Chest lifted. Chin up. Weight distributed evenly. The posture should feel stable, not teetering. Hold for 20-40 seconds — long enough for the nervous system to register the new configuration.

**Common failure modes:**
- Making the pose silly or exaggerated (if the child laughs through it, the somatic shift doesn't register)
- Not holding long enough (the nervous system needs 15+ seconds to read the new postural signal)
- Holding breath during the pose (common — remind the child to breathe while standing tall)
- Rigid rather than expansive (the pose should feel OPEN, not BRACED)

**Therapeutic mechanism:**
Levine: the body and brain are in constant bidirectional communication. Defensive postures (curled, contracted, small) signal threat to the nervous system, maintaining sympathetic or dorsal vagal activation. Expansive postures signal safety and agency, promoting ventral vagal engagement. The posture doesn't create a feeling — it creates the PHYSIOLOGICAL CONDITIONS under which the feeling can emerge. "The body leads, the mind follows."

**Pacing notes:**
Slow entry into the pose. 5 seconds to set feet. 5 seconds to open arms. 5 seconds to lift chest. Then HOLD — 20-30 seconds minimum. The settling-into is important: each body adjustment is a step toward full expansion.

**Cautions:**
- Some children may feel exposed or vulnerable in an open posture — this IS the therapeutic edge, but don't force it. "Make yourself as big as feels OK right now."
- Physical limitations: some children cannot raise arms overhead. Wide stance + chest lift is sufficient.
- The transition FROM the pose matters — don't collapse afterward. Slowly, deliberately return to neutral while maintaining the felt sense.

### 2. Porges (Polyvagal Theory)

**Guided practice sequences:**
Not directly provided. Porges's contribution: the autonomic nervous system reads postural configuration as a survival signal. Contracted posture = defensive = sympathetic/dorsal activation. Expanded posture = safe/powerful = ventral vagal activation. The Hero Stand exploits this by using posture as the input to shift autonomic state.

**Therapeutic mechanism:**
Porges: postural expansion activates the social engagement system (ventral vagal). The facial muscles relax, the voice becomes prosodic, the heart rate variability increases. The child doesn't just LOOK brave — their nervous system actually shifts toward a state of calm-with-energy, which is genuine courage: not the absence of fear, but the presence of agency alongside fear.

**Cautions:**
- The environment must feel safe enough to support an open posture. An anxious child in an unsafe-feeling environment will not be able to sustain expansion — the defensive system will override.
- M29's narrative context (trusted characters present, family watching, Grand Warden's approval) provides multiple safety cues. This is therapeutically necessary.

### 3. Kaiser Greenland (Inner Kids)

**Guided practice sequences:**
Kaiser Greenland's movement-based courage practices for children: (a) "Warrior pose" — wide stance, arms extended, gaze forward. (b) "Mountain pose" — feet together, spine tall, arms at sides or raised, imagining being a mountain. (c) Hold for several breaths while noticing body sensations. (d) "How does it feel to stand like this?"

**Child-specific language:**
"Stand like a hero." "Make yourself as tall as you can." "Feel your feet on the ground — really feel them." "Now open your arms wide — like you're holding something huge." "How does your body feel right now?" Kaiser Greenland emphasizes the INQUIRY after the pose: the child reflects on the felt shift.

**Physical cues:**
For 7-10 year olds: feet apart (not too wide — shoulder width), arms out to the sides or raised, head up. Hold for 3-5 breaths (approximately 15-25 seconds). Eyes can be open — this is a movement module.

**Common failure modes:**
- Goofiness overriding the practice (especially with younger children)
- Rushing through without holding (the hold IS the practice)
- Forgetting the inquiry ("How does it feel?") — without reflection, it's just a pose

**Therapeutic mechanism:**
Kaiser Greenland: embodied practices work for children because children experience their world through their bodies before their minds. A child can't think themselves brave. But they CAN stand brave — and the standing teaches the feeling.

**Pacing notes:**
Moderate — children can hold poses for 15-25 seconds if engaged. Beyond 30 seconds may trigger restlessness.

**Cautions:**
- Frame as "strong" not "aggressive" — power without hostility
- Some children may feel self-conscious standing in an exposed pose — normalize it: "This might feel a little silly at first. That's OK. Just feel what happens."

### 4. van der Kolk (The Body Keeps the Score)

**Guided practice sequences:**
Van der Kolk does not provide specific Hero Stand scripts. His contribution: when the body is stuck in a defensive pattern (freeze, collapse), cognitive interventions ("just be brave") fail because the body's alarm system overrides cognition. The body must be moved OUT of the defensive pattern through physical action.

**Therapeutic mechanism:**
Van der Kolk: "Traumatized people need to learn that they can tolerate their sensations, befriend their inner experiences, and cultivate new action patterns." The Hero Stand IS a new action pattern — it gives the body an alternative to contraction. For non-traumatized anxious children: the same principle applies on a smaller scale. The body's habitual response to fear is contraction; the Hero Stand teaches expansion as an available response.

**Cautions:**
- Some children's defensive patterns are deeply ingrained — a single Hero Stand won't override them. The practice is planting a seed, not a complete intervention.
- Physical expansion should be INVITED, not demanded.

### 5. Chödrön (When Things Fall Apart)

**Guided practice sequences:**
Chödrön does not publish movement exercises. Her contribution is philosophical: the willingness to remain open when everything says close. "Rather than letting our negativity get the better of us, we could acknowledge that right now we feel like a piece of shit and not be squeamish about taking a good look." For M29: Tessa's openness at the cliff edge IS Chödrön's teaching made physical.

**Therapeutic mechanism:**
Chödrön: courage is not the absence of fear. It is the willingness to remain present with fear without acting on the impulse to flee. The Hero Stand embodies this: the body is open, exposed, NOT fleeing — and the fear is still there. Both exist simultaneously.

**Cautions:**
- Do not frame the Hero Stand as overcoming fear. Frame it as standing WITH fear while choosing openness.

---

### 6. Thich Nhat Hanh

**Guided practice sequences:**
Thich Nhat Hanh's mindful movement: awareness during physical action. "When you stand, know that you are standing." For the Hero Stand: full awareness of each physical element — the feet, the legs, the torso, the arms, the breath — while in the expanded posture.

**Therapeutic mechanism:**
Mindful movement combines the body-shift mechanism (Levine, Porges) with present-moment awareness. The child is not just standing tall — they are AWARE of standing tall. This awareness-during-action is the bridge between movement practices and stillness practices.

**Pacing notes:**
Thich Nhat Hanh's movement guidance is very slow, deliberate, each action named. "Lifting... opening... standing..." This pacing matches the Hero Stand's entry sequence.

## Cross-Source Synthesis

### Points of Agreement

1. **Body posture directly influences emotional state.** All somatic sources (Levine, Porges, van der Kolk) agree: the body is not just expressing courage, it is CREATING the conditions for courage.
2. **Expansion is the therapeutic opposite of defensive contraction.** Universal across sources.
3. **The hold is the practice, not the entry.** Levine, Kaiser Greenland, Porges: the nervous system needs sustained exposure to the new posture (15+ seconds) to register the shift.
4. **Inquiry after the pose deepens the learning.** Kaiser Greenland, Levine: "How does your body feel now?" converts physical experience to conscious awareness.
5. **Courage includes fear, not its absence.** Chödrön, Levine, van der Kolk: the Hero Stand is not about not being scared — it's about being scared AND standing big.

### Points of Tension

1. **Silly or serious?** Kaiser Greenland allows playful entry; Levine emphasizes genuine somatic shift. Resolution: playful entry is fine (this is a children's app), but the HOLD must be genuine. Pip can be funny; the pose is real.
2. **Eyes open or closed?** Movement techniques typically use open eyes. The settling period after the pose may close eyes. Resolution: eyes open during the Hero Stand, soft or closed during the settling.

### Recommended Approach

Movement-module structure (per inventory guidelines):

1. **Phase B entry** (5 seconds) — "Stand up. Feel your feet on the ground."
2. **Set the base** (5 seconds) — "Feet apart. Stand steady."
3. **Expand** (8 seconds) — "Now — open your arms wide. As wide as they'll go. Lift your chest. Head up. Make yourself as BIG as you can."
4. **Hold** (20-25 seconds) — "Stay right there. Feel how big you are. Feel your feet holding you. Feel your arms stretching out. Feel your chest wide open. Breathe in — and breathe out. Your body is taking up ALL the space. How does that feel?"
5. **Settle** (30 seconds) — "Slowly bring your arms down. Sit or stand comfortably. Close your eyes. Feel what's different. The bigness is still inside you."
6. **Landing** — "Your body just taught you something. You can feel that anytime."

Total: ~70-80 seconds (movement + settling). Active posture ~35 seconds. Settling ~30 seconds.

### Elements to Incorporate

- **Feet-first grounding** (Levine, Kaiser Greenland) — stable base before expansion
- **Progressive expansion** — feet → arms → chest → head (staged, not all at once)
- **Sustained hold with breath cue** (Levine, Porges) — breathe DURING the pose
- **"How does that feel?" inquiry** (Kaiser Greenland) — convert body experience to awareness
- **Settling period with eyes closed** (movement-module flow) — integrate the shift
- **"The bigness is still inside you" landing** — the felt sense persists after the pose

### Elements to Avoid

- **"Pretend to be brave" framing** (all sources — the posture creates genuine shift, not performance)
- **Aggressive/combative language** ("fight your fear!") — expansion is openness, not opposition
- **Rushing through the hold** (Levine, Porges — 15+ seconds needed for nervous system registration)
- **Collapsing after the pose** — the transition to settling must be deliberate, not a release
- **Telling the child they ARE brave** — let the body teach the feeling
- **Seated alternative** (this technique requires standing — the full-body expansion is the mechanism)
