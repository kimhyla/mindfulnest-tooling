## PURPOSE

The original Technique Inventory & Gap Analysis v1 surveyed all 26 clinical sources but systematically under-counted movement techniques. The feasibility filter ("eyes-closed, 60–120 seconds, seated") accidentally excluded most movement. That filter is now updated:

> **Updated feasibility filter:** "Feasible within the Phase B format, INCLUDING the movement-module flow. The child can stand, move, balance, stretch, squeeze, shake, rock, fold, push, or hold poses. They end in stillness. Eyes can be open during movement, closed during settling."

This document builds the movement wing of the technique inventory that was accidentally omitted from v1. It does NOT reassign existing techniques, assign techniques to specific module numbers, or write Phase B scripts. It establishes what's available.

---

## MOVEMENT-MODULE FLOW (From Bible v10)

For all movement techniques in this inventory, the module structure is:

**Phase A:** Interactive demo of poses/movements (child watches, learns)
**Phase B:** Guided movement sequence (child follows along) → 30–45 seconds of guided settling/body scan
**Rescue:** Child sustains the settled, body-aware state AFTER movement while creature responds

Phase B audio may be slightly longer for movement modules (60–90 seconds vs 45–60) to allow genuine settling time. The child ends in stillness. The relaxation response AFTER movement is where the therapeutic benefit lands. The child notices the contrast between active and still.

## ENVIRONMENTAL CONSTRAINTS

- **Space:** ~4×4 feet. Bedroom, living room, or therapy office. No outdoor space required.
- **Age 7–10:** No complex balance sequences, no poses requiring significant upper body strength, no poses requiring specific flexibility. Simple, clear, one-instruction-at-a-time movements.
- **One pose OR one short sequence (2–3 movements) per module.** Not a yoga class.
- **Device context:** The child's device (tablet/phone) is visible during Phase A but may be set aside or held during Phase B. Audio-guided movement means the child doesn't need to watch the screen.

## TECHNIQUE ENTRIES BY DOMAIN

### DOMAIN 1: BREATH AWARENESS (Tessa — Turtle)

**Domain scope:** Breathing as a tool. Movement techniques in this domain use the body to make breath MORE VISIBLE, MORE FELT, or MORE RHYTHMIC. The movement serves the breath — not the other way around.

---

#### BA-M1: Breath-Synchronized Arm Raises ("Balloon Arms")

**Clinical name:** Breath-synchronized movement / Pranayama with movement
**Kid-friendly working name:** Balloon Arms

**Description:** The child stands with arms at their sides. On the inhale, they slowly raise both arms out and up overhead (like inflating a balloon). On the exhale, they slowly lower arms back to sides. The movement is timed to the breath — arms rise with inhale, fall with exhale. After 4–6 cycles, the child stands still and notices how their breath feels without the movement cue.

**Sources:**
- Kabat-Zinn (MBSR yoga — breath-synchronized movement is a core MBSR element; standing poses with breath as the anchor)
- Thich Nhat Hanh (movement as breath awareness — "breathing in, I raise my arms; breathing out, I lower my arms"; simplest possible instruction)
- Kaiser Greenland (Inner Kids — arm movements synchronized to breath as age-appropriate practice for children; makes breath "visible" through the body)
- Generation Mindful (breath-body connection activities for ages 5–10)

**Domain assignment:** Breath Awareness (Tessa). The movement exists SOLELY to make breath more tangible. The arms are a visual/kinesthetic cue for inhale-exhale rhythm. The settling portion returns to breath-only awareness — the arms taught the breath, then the training wheels come off.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike M1 (belly breathing, seated, hands on tummy) and M2 (4-7-8, seated, counting), this uses WHOLE-BODY movement to externalize the breath rhythm. The child sees and feels their breath through their arms, not just their belly. Different anchor, different mechanism.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Arms-up-arms-down is natural for all ages 7–10. No balance challenge. No strength requirement. No flexibility demand. Can be done in pajamas, in any 2×2 foot space.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (4–6 breath-synchronized arm cycles) takes ~40–50 seconds. Settling portion: "Now let your arms rest at your sides… stand still… notice how your breath keeps going all on its own, even without your arms helping…" (30–40 seconds). Total ~80–90 seconds. Fits comfortably.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Multiple approved sources. Perfectly age-appropriate. Clean domain fit. Clear distinctiveness from M1/M2. Natural settling transition.

---

#### BA-M2: Breath with Forward Fold ("Waterfall Breath")

**Clinical name:** Standing forward fold with exhale / Uttanasana breath integration
**Kid-friendly working name:** Waterfall Breath

**Description:** The child stands with feet hip-width apart. On the inhale, they reach arms overhead. On the exhale, they fold forward, letting arms and head hang heavy like a waterfall. They hold the fold for one breath, feeling gravity pull. On the next inhale, they slowly roll up to standing. Repeat 3–4 times. The settling portion is standing still, eyes closed, noticing the rush of blood and warmth in the body from the folds.

**Sources:**
- Kabat-Zinn (MBSR yoga — forward fold as breath-anchored movement; emphasis on "letting gravity do the work")
- van der Kolk (The Body Keeps the Score — forward fold activates parasympathetic response through head-below-heart position; vagal stimulation through positional change)
- Kaiser Greenland (adapted yoga poses for children — "ragdoll" as age-appropriate forward fold)
- Levine (releasing held tension through gravity-assisted movement; "letting the body go" as pendulation cue)

**Domain assignment:** Breath Awareness (Tessa). The inhale drives the rise, the exhale drives the fold. Breath controls the movement. The physiological bonus (parasympathetic activation from head-below-heart) reinforces Tessa's calming territory. But the child's EXPERIENCE is: "my breath moves my body up and down."

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike BA-M1 (arms only, upright), this uses gravity and inversion. The fold creates a strong physical sensation — head rush, stretch in hamstrings, blood flow shift — that makes the settling contrast vivid. Different kinesthetic signature from arms-up/arms-down.

**Age appropriateness:** Good with one caution. The forward fold itself is natural (kids bend over constantly). However, children under 8 may need explicit instruction to keep knees soft (not locked). "Let your knees be a little bendy" solves this. No flexibility requirement — they fold as far as feels comfortable. No balance issue (feet stay planted).

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (3–4 fold cycles) takes ~50–60 seconds. Settling: "Now stand tall one more time… feel your feet on the floor… close your eyes… notice how your body feels alive and warm…" (30–35 seconds). Total ~80–95 seconds. Fits, but at the upper edge. May need only 3 cycles.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Well-supported by multiple sources. Physiologically interesting (parasympathetic activation through inversion). Clean domain fit. Distinct from other breath techniques. Minor age adaptation needed (soft knees), but simple.

#### BA-M3: Humming Breath with Body Vibration ("Buzzy Bee Body")

**Clinical name:** Bhramari pranayama with somatic awareness
**Kid-friendly working name:** Buzzy Bee Body

**Description:** The child stands or sits. They take a deep breath in, then hum on the exhale — like a bumblebee. While humming, they place their hands on different body parts (throat, chest, belly) to FEEL the vibration. After 4–5 hum cycles, they stand/sit still and notice the buzzy, tingly feeling in their body. The movement element is the hand placement — actively exploring WHERE the hum vibrates.

**Sources:**
- Thich Nhat Hanh (sound-based calming practices; humming as breath extension)
- Porges (Polyvagal Theory — laryngeal vibration stimulates vagus nerve through a different pathway than exhale extension alone; the hum engages the pharyngeal and laryngeal branches)
- Kaiser Greenland (Inner Kids — "buzzing bee breath" as named children's practice)
- Benson (monotonous sound repetition as relaxation response trigger)

**Domain assignment:** Breath Awareness (Tessa). The hum IS a breath technique — it's a structured exhale with vibration added. The hand placement adds a movement/interoceptive layer but serves breath awareness: "feel where your breath buzzes." This was already identified as C-1 in the seated inventory but the hand-placement version has a genuine movement component.

**Distinctiveness:** This is the movement-enhanced version of C-1 (Humming/Buzzing Breath) from the original inventory. The seated version uses humming alone. This version adds active hand placement as a kinesthetic exploration of where breath vibrates. If C-1 already occupies a module slot as a seated technique, this variant may be redundant. However, the hand-exploration element makes it more body-engaged than the seated version.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Kids love making buzzing sounds. The hand-on-body exploration is simple and fascinating for this age group. No balance, strength, or flexibility requirements.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (4–5 hum cycles with hand shifts) takes ~40–50 seconds. Settling: "Now let your hands rest… close your eyes… notice that tingly, buzzy feeling still humming inside you…" (30 seconds). Total ~70–80 seconds. Comfortable fit.

**Strength rating: BORDERLINE.** The movement element (hand placement) is genuine but minimal — this sits at the boundary between "movement module" and "seated module with a movement component." If C-1 is assigned as a seated module, this variant may be too similar. If movement distribution requires a Breath Awareness entry, this works. Best used if C-1 is NOT already a separate module.

### DOMAIN 2: PRESENT-MOMENT AWARENESS + METACOGNITION (Luna — Owl)

**Domain scope:** Attention, noticing, observing. Movement techniques in this domain use movement as the OBJECT of attention — the child notices what their body is doing with focused, deliberate awareness.

---

#### PM-M1: Slow-Motion Walking ("Owl Steps")

**Clinical name:** Mindful walking / Walking meditation
**Kid-friendly working name:** Owl Steps

**Description:** The child walks VERY slowly across a small space (4–6 steps). They notice every micro-movement: lifting the foot, moving it forward, placing it down, shifting weight. Each step might take 5–8 seconds. After crossing the space (or walking in a small circle for 4–6 steps), they stand still and notice what it feels like to NOT be moving — the sudden awareness of stillness.

**Sources:**
- Thich Nhat Hanh (walking meditation — primary source; "walk as if your feet are kissing the earth"; the simplest and most established mindful movement practice)
- Kabat-Zinn (MBSR walking meditation — "feel each part of the step: lifting, moving, placing"; formal walking practice as core MBSR component)
- Kaiser Greenland (mindful walking adapted for children — shorter distances, more verbal guidance, "detective feet" framing)
- Levine (grounding through movement — feet-on-ground contact as nervous system regulation)

**Domain assignment:** Present-Moment Awareness (Luna). The skill being taught is NOTICING — granular, deliberate attention to what's happening right now in the body. The walking isn't exercise or relaxation; it's an attention training vehicle. "Can you notice every tiny part of one single step?" That's a metacognitive skill — observing your own movement with precision.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike M3 (observing thoughts) and M4 (sustaining attention on sound), this observes PHYSICAL MOVEMENT. The attention anchor is the child's own body in motion. Unlike Bramble's body awareness techniques (which notice internal signals), this notices MOVEMENT MECHANICS — the external, observable act of walking, broken into granular steps.

**Age appropriateness:** Good. Walking is universal. The challenge is SLOWNESS — 7-year-olds may find extreme slow motion difficult or silly. Framing matters: "Like an owl moving through the night forest, so quiet and careful that you don't rustle a single leaf" gives purpose to the slowness. 3–4 steps may be enough for younger children.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (6–8 slow steps) takes ~40–50 seconds. Settling: "Now stop… stand very still like the owl has landed on a branch… feel your feet holding you… notice what stillness feels like after all that careful moving…" (30–40 seconds). Total ~70–90 seconds. Good fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Walking meditation is one of the most clinically validated mindful movement practices. Multiple primary sources. Excellent fit for Present-Moment Awareness domain. Clear distinctiveness from existing modules.

#### PM-M2: Freeze-and-Notice ("Statue Game")

**Clinical name:** Mindful freeze / Stop-and-observe practice
**Kid-friendly working name:** Statue Game

**Description:** The child moves freely (gentle shaking, swaying, marching in place) for 5–8 seconds, then FREEZES on an audio cue (bell or Bird's voice). While frozen, they scan their body with attention: "What is your body doing right now? Where are your arms? What do your legs feel? Where is your weight?" After 8–10 seconds of noticing, they move again. Repeat 3–4 cycles. The settling portion extends the final freeze into a longer body-awareness moment.

**Sources:**
- Kaiser Greenland (Inner Kids — "freeze games" as core children's mindfulness practice; alternating movement and stillness to train the "noticing" muscle)
- Kabat-Zinn (the moment of stopping as mindfulness; the pause between is where awareness lives)
- Shanker (Self-Reg — alternating arousal states teaches regulation; the child learns they can shift between active and still)
- Hawn/MindUP ("brain breaks" that alternate movement and stillness to engage executive function)

**Domain assignment:** Present-Moment Awareness (Luna). The freeze moments are the practice. The child is learning to OBSERVE their own body state with accuracy — "what is happening RIGHT NOW?" The movement is just the setup that creates something interesting to notice. The metacognitive skill is the snapshot: capturing what's true in this moment without judgment.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike PM-M1 (slow continuous movement), this alternates between movement and stillness. The teaching happens in the CONTRAST — the abrupt shift from moving to frozen creates a heightened awareness window. Unlike seated attention practices (M3, M4), the child has a body-in-motion to observe, not thoughts or sounds.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. This is essentially a structured version of "freeze dance" — a game 7-year-olds already know and love. The clinical layer (guided body scanning during freeze) adds the therapeutic content to a familiar format. Very high engagement potential.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (3–4 move-freeze cycles) takes ~50–60 seconds. The final freeze extends into settling: "This time, stay frozen… but keep your detective eyes turned inward… feel your heartbeat… feel your breathing… notice what your body chose to do when it froze…" (30 seconds). Total ~80–90 seconds. Good fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Kaiser Greenland explicitly recommends this format for children. High engagement. Clean domain fit. Builds naturally on children's existing play vocabulary. The alternation structure gives the settling transition a natural on-ramp.

#### PM-M3: Balance Pose with Attention Focus ("Still Point")

**Clinical name:** Single-leg balance with focused attention
**Kid-friendly working name:** Still Point

**Description:** The child stands on one foot (the other foot resting lightly against the ankle or just lifted slightly off the ground). They hold the balance for 10–15 seconds while noticing everything their body does to stay upright — tiny ankle adjustments, core engagement, arms shifting. Then switch feet. The practice makes invisible micro-movements VISIBLE through the challenge of balance. The settling portion: both feet down, eyes closed, noticing how the body feels after concentrating so hard.

**Sources:**
- Kaiser Greenland (balance as attention training for children — "your body has to pay total attention to stay balanced; that's exactly what mindfulness is")
- Kabat-Zinn (MBSR yoga — tree pose and balance poses as present-moment anchors; "you cannot balance while thinking about tomorrow")
- Shapiro (IAA framework — balance demands Intention + Attention + Attitude simultaneously; a pure form of mindful awareness)

**Domain assignment:** Present-Moment Awareness (Luna). Balance DEMANDS present-moment attention — it's impossible to balance while mentally elsewhere. The child discovers that their body is already doing sophisticated real-time processing (micro-adjustments) that they've never noticed before. That's metacognition: "my body was doing all of this without me knowing."

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike PM-M1 (walking — forward motion) and PM-M2 (freeze — alternation), this uses STILLNESS-UNDER-CHALLENGE. The body is working hard but barely moving. The attention object is incredibly subtle — tiny wobbles and corrections. This is the most internally focused of the Present-Moment movement techniques.

**Age appropriateness:** Moderate concern. Single-leg balance varies widely at ages 7–10. Some 7-year-olds can hold it for 30 seconds; others wobble immediately. CRITICAL DESIGN CHOICE: the instruction must frame wobbling as SUCCESS, not failure. "Feel all those tiny wiggles? THAT is your body paying attention! Every wobble is your body making a smart decision." Offer a wall-touch option for stability. Keep balance duration short (10–15 seconds per side).

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (10–15 seconds per foot, two rounds) takes ~50–60 seconds including transitions. Settling: "Now both feet down, flat on the floor… close your eyes… notice how QUIET your body feels now that it doesn't have to work so hard to balance…" (30 seconds). Total ~80–90 seconds. Fits well.

**Strength rating: STRONG with caveat.** Clinically sound and excellent domain fit, but requires careful scaffolding for children who struggle with balance. The "wobbling is success" framing is essential. If that framing is nailed, this is therapeutically powerful — it teaches children that their body is constantly, brilliantly processing without their conscious knowledge.

### DOMAIN 3: COURAGE (Benson — Bunny)

**Domain scope:** Approaching rather than avoiding. Willingness to feel discomfort. Movement techniques in this domain use the body to practice APPROACH — physically moving toward something uncomfortable, or holding a position that requires effort and persistence.

---

#### CO-M1: Power Pose / Brave Stance ("Hero Stand")

**Clinical name:** Expansive postural positioning / Approach-oriented body posture
**Kid-friendly working name:** Hero Stand

**Description:** The child stands tall — feet wide, hands on hips (or arms raised in a V). They hold this expansive, open posture for 15–20 seconds while the Guide Bird narrates what's happening: "Your body is telling your brain: I'm ready. I can handle this." Then they try a second pose: feet planted, arms crossed firmly over chest (protective/strong, not closed/scared). The settling portion has them return to a neutral standing position, eyes closed, noticing how their body feels after standing like a hero.

**Sources:**
- van der Kolk (posture affects emotional state — body-to-brain pathway; "when you change the body, you change the brain"; expansive postures reduce cortisol and increase approach behavior)
- Chödrön (the practice of staying OPEN rather than closing down; physical openness as metaphor for emotional openness to difficulty)
- Levine (Somatic Experiencing — body posture as resource anchor; practicing "I can handle this" through the body before the mind believes it)
- Porges (ventral vagal engagement through upright, open posture; neuroception of safety supported by expansive body position)

**Domain assignment:** Courage (Benson). The pose IS courage practice — the child physically rehearses what "brave" feels like in the body. This is approach-behavior embodied. The body learns "open and strong" before the mind catches up. Distinct from emotional courage (M7 approaching fear mentally) because this is PHYSICAL courage — the body leading the brain.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike M7 (mental approach to fear through visualization) and M8 (naming emotions verbally), this works entirely through body posture. No cognitive content — pure somatic approach. The mechanism is bottom-up (body→brain), not top-down (thought→feeling).

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. "Stand like a superhero" is immediately understood by every child in this age range. No balance challenge, no strength requirement, no flexibility demand. The poses are simple and fun. High engagement.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (two poses, 15–20 seconds each, with brief transition) takes ~40–50 seconds. Settling: "Now relax your arms… stand normally… close your eyes… but keep that brave feeling inside… notice where you feel it — your chest? Your shoulders? That's your body remembering what brave feels like…" (30–40 seconds). Total ~70–90 seconds. Excellent fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Well-supported by multiple sources on the body-to-brain pathway. Perfect age-appropriate engagement. Clean domain fit. Clear distinctiveness from existing courage techniques.

---

#### CO-M2: Shake-It-Off / Tension Discharge ("Fear Shake")

**Clinical name:** Somatic tremoring / Neurogenic tremor discharge
**Kid-friendly working name:** Fear Shake

**Description:** The child shakes their whole body — starting with hands (shake shake shake), then arms, then shoulders, then whole body wiggling and bouncing for 15–20 seconds. They shake as if they're shaking off something they don't want — like a dog after a bath. Then they STOP and stand completely still. The contrast between shaking and stillness is the therapeutic moment. The settling portion notices what the body feels like AFTER the discharge.

**Sources:**
- Levine (Somatic Experiencing — this is Levine's PRIMARY contribution; animals discharge stress through tremoring and shaking after threat; humans suppress this natural discharge; completing the stress cycle through intentional shaking restores regulation)
- van der Kolk (the body needs to complete stress responses; shaking/tremoring as natural trauma resolution; "the body keeps the score" until the stress cycle completes)
- Shanker (Self-Reg — physical discharge as biological domain regulation; the body needs to release activation before cognitive regulation is possible)
- Kaiser Greenland ("shake it out" as children's regulation practice; developmentally natural for ages 7–10)

**Domain assignment:** Courage (Benson). The shake is approach-behavior toward bodily activation. Instead of SUPPRESSING the jittery, scared feeling (which children naturally do), this says: "Feel scared? Feel shaky? GOOD — let it OUT. Shake it right out of your body." The courage is in ALLOWING the physical expression of fear rather than clamping down. This teaches that fear has a beginning, middle, and END — and you can help it finish.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike CO-M1 (static power pose — holding strength), this is DYNAMIC discharge — releasing held energy. Opposite movement quality: CO-M1 is stillness-as-strength; CO-M2 is movement-as-release. Different mechanism: CO-M1 is body-to-brain posture signaling; CO-M2 is completing the stress cycle through motor discharge.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Shaking is instinctive and fun. Children don't need instruction — they need PERMISSION. "Shake your whole body like a wet dog!" is immediately engaging. No coordination, balance, or strength requirements.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (progressive shaking sequence, 20–25 seconds of active shaking) takes ~30–40 seconds including ramping up and ramping down. Settling: "Now stop… stand completely still… feel that? Your body just did something really brave — it let the scared feeling finish instead of holding it in… notice how different you feel now…" (30–40 seconds). Total ~60–80 seconds. Perfect fit, actually on the shorter side which allows generous settling time.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Levine's stress discharge is the single most clinically distinctive contribution of Somatic Experiencing. This is its purest child-appropriate expression. Clean domain fit. High engagement. Natural settling contrast.

#### CO-M3: Wall Push / Isometric Resistance ("Strong Push")

**Clinical name:** Isometric resistance / Proprioceptive input for emotional regulation
**Kid-friendly working name:** Strong Push

**Description:** The child stands facing a wall and pushes against it with both hands, as hard as they can, for 10–15 seconds. They push like they're trying to move the wall. Then release and step back. Repeat once more. The push creates intense proprioceptive feedback — the body engages fully without going anywhere. The settling portion notices the contrast: "Your muscles just pushed as hard as they could, and now they're resting. Feel the difference."

**Sources:**
- Levine (isometric resistance as pendulation tool — the body engages intensely then releases, teaching the nervous system to oscillate between activation and settling)
- van der Kolk (proprioceptive input as regulatory tool; body-based approaches for children who struggle with top-down regulation)
- Shanker (biological domain regulation through intense proprioceptive input; used in occupational therapy contexts for self-regulation)
- Saltzman (body-based regulation exercises for children — push/pull activities as calming tools)

**Domain assignment:** Courage (Benson). Pushing against something immovable is a metaphor for courage that the BODY understands. The child practices engaging fully with something difficult — pushing with all their strength against resistance — and then letting go. The lesson: you can put your whole self into something hard, and you'll be OK afterward. That's the approach template for emotional courage.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike CO-M1 (static posture, no exertion) and CO-M2 (dynamic shaking, release-focused), this is EFFORTFUL ENGAGEMENT against resistance. Maximum muscular effort. The therapeutic mechanism is proprioceptive input + effort→release contrast, not posture signaling or stress discharge.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Pushing against a wall is intuitive and safe. No balance risk (wall provides stability). No strength concern (every child pushes as hard as THEY can). Requires a wall within arm's reach — universally available in bedroom, therapy office, or living room.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (two push cycles of 10–15 seconds each, with transition) takes ~35–45 seconds. Settling: "Step away from the wall… let your arms hang… close your eyes… feel how warm and alive your muscles are… they just did something really hard, and now they get to rest…" (30–40 seconds). Total ~65–85 seconds. Excellent fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Well-supported through proprioceptive regulation literature. Universally feasible. Clean domain fit. Strong distinctiveness from other courage movement techniques. The effort→release contrast provides a natural settling transition that's physiologically robust.

### DOMAIN 4: KINDNESS (Ember — Fox)

**Domain scope:** Self-compassion, compassion for others, loving-kindness. Movement techniques in this domain use the body to practice WARMTH — physical gestures of care, comfort, and tenderness directed inward or outward.

---

#### KI-M1: Self-Hug / Butterfly Embrace ("Warm Wings")

**Clinical name:** Bilateral self-touch / Self-compassion embodiment (Butterfly Hug variant)
**Kid-friendly working name:** Warm Wings

**Description:** The child crosses their arms over their chest, placing each hand on the opposite shoulder. They give themselves a gentle squeeze — a self-hug. While holding the hug, they alternate tapping each shoulder slowly (left-right-left-right, ~1 tap per second) for 15–20 seconds. This is the "butterfly hug" adapted for MindfulNest. The settling portion has them keep their hands on their shoulders, stop tapping, and notice the warmth of their own hands on their body.

**Sources:**
- Neff (self-compassion physical practices — self-touch as embodied self-kindness; placing hands on the body activates the caregiving system and releases oxytocin)
- Germer (Mindful Self-Compassion — the physical component of self-compassion practice; "soothing touch" as a core MSC exercise)
- van der Kolk (bilateral stimulation as calming mechanism; self-touch as body-based safety signal)
- Porges (touch activates the ventral vagal "safe" circuit; self-touch works through the same pathway as being comforted by another)

**Domain assignment:** Kindness (Ember). The self-hug IS self-compassion in physical form — the child is literally holding themselves with care. The bilateral tapping adds a calming layer, but the primary teaching is: "You can comfort yourself. Your own arms are enough." This is Ember's territory: warmth directed inward.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike M5 (mental loving-kindness meditation — visualizing warmth) and M6 (imagined apology/repair process), this is PHYSICAL self-compassion. No cognitive content, no visualization, no words. The body practices kindness directly. The mechanism is somatic (oxytocin, vagal activation through touch) rather than cognitive (metta phrases).

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Self-hugging is natural and comfortable for 7–10-year-olds. The butterfly tapping adds a simple pattern. No balance, strength, or flexibility issues. Some children may feel self-conscious about hugging themselves — framing as "the fox's special warmth wrap" provides narrative cover.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (self-hug + 15–20 seconds of tapping) takes ~25–35 seconds. Settling: "Now keep your arms right there, but stop the tapping… just hold yourself gently… feel the warmth of your hands on your shoulders… that warmth is YOUR kindness, and it's always there when you need it…" (30–40 seconds). Total ~55–75 seconds. Excellent fit — actually allows extra settling time.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Neff and Germer explicitly endorse physical self-compassion practices. van der Kolk validates the mechanism. Clean domain fit. High distinctiveness from existing kindness modules. Simple and deeply age-appropriate.

---

#### KI-M2: Gentle Rocking / Self-Soothing Rhythm ("Comfort Rock")

**Clinical name:** Vestibular self-soothing / Rhythmic self-regulation
**Kid-friendly working name:** Comfort Rock

**Description:** The child sits on the floor (cross-legged or knees hugged to chest) and gently rocks forward and back, or side to side, at a slow comfortable rhythm — like being rocked in a rocking chair. The rocking is slow and gentle, not vigorous. After 20–30 seconds of rocking, they gradually slow down and become still, noticing how the rocking feeling continues inside even after they stop.

**Sources:**
- van der Kolk (rhythmic movement as self-regulation; vestibular input activates parasympathetic nervous system; rocking is a natural self-soothing behavior from infancy — clinical practice reclaims it deliberately)
- Levine (pendulation — rhythmic oscillation as the body's natural settling mechanism; gentle rocking teaches the nervous system to find its own rhythm)
- Porges (rhythmic vestibular stimulation engages the ventral vagal circuit; rocking chair effect on autonomic regulation)
- Saltzman (gentle movement practices for children — rhythmic, repetitive, calming)

**Domain assignment:** Kindness (Ember). Rocking is the body's earliest experience of being CARED FOR — infants are rocked by caregivers. When a child rocks themselves, they're activating the same neural circuitry as being comforted by another person. This is self-kindness through the body's oldest comfort language. The message: "You know how to comfort yourself. You've known since before you could talk."

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike KI-M1 (self-touch, arms on body), this is VESTIBULAR — the whole body moves rhythmically. Different sensory pathway (vestibular vs. tactile). Different movement quality (oscillation vs. holding/tapping). The rocking targets a deeper, more primitive self-soothing system.

**Age appropriateness:** Good. Rocking is instinctive. However, some children in this age range may view rocking as "babyish." Framing matters: "This is how foxes settle their cubs — back and forth, slow and warm." Alternatively, framing it as "the way the earth breathes — tides in, tides out" removes the infant association entirely. Sitting on the floor is needed — easily done in any setting.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (20–30 seconds of gentle rocking) takes ~25–35 seconds. Settling: "Now let the rocking slow down… slower and slower… and stop… but notice — the rocking feeling keeps going inside you, like a wave that hasn't quite finished yet… that's your body's way of being kind to itself…" (30–40 seconds). Total ~55–75 seconds. Good fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Well-supported by vestibular regulation literature. Deeply distinctive mechanism. Clean Kindness domain fit with the right framing. The potential "babyish" concern is manageable through narrative framing.

#### KI-M3: Heart-Hand Breathing ("Warm Hands, Kind Heart")

**Clinical name:** Compassionate self-touch with breath / Soothing touch practice
**Kid-friendly working name:** Warm Hands, Kind Heart

**Description:** The child rubs their palms together vigorously for 5–10 seconds until their hands feel warm. Then they place one warm hand on their heart and one on their belly. They breathe slowly, feeling the warmth of their own hands on their body. After 4–5 breaths with hands in place, they slowly lower their hands and notice the lingering warmth.

**Sources:**
- Neff (self-compassion physical practices — hand on heart as core self-kindness gesture; warmth as embodied compassion)
- Germer (Mindful Self-Compassion — "soothing touch" with warm hands as specific practice)
- Porges (self-touch as safety signal; warmth on body as ventral vagal cue)
- Kaiser Greenland (adapting hand-on-heart for children; the hand-rubbing warmth makes the gesture more tangible for young children)

**Domain assignment:** Kindness (Ember). This is a variant of C-4 (Compassionate Self-Touch / Hand on Heart) from the original inventory, which was flagged as BORDERLINE with the note "domain assignment depends on framing." With the Kindness framing — "you're warming your own heart with your own hands" — the domain fit is clean. The warmth is the kindness. The gesture is the compassion.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike KI-M1 (shoulder self-hug, bilateral tapping), this targets the HEART space specifically and uses GENERATED warmth (from hand rubbing). Unlike KI-M2 (vestibular rocking), this is still and tactile. The warmth-generation step (rubbing hands) makes the kindness feel like something the child actively CREATED, not just received.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Rubbing hands together is a natural, familiar gesture. Placing warm hands on body is simple. No balance, strength, or flexibility requirements. Children are often fascinated by the warmth they can generate with their own hands.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (hand rubbing ~10 seconds + hand placement + 4–5 breaths) takes ~35–45 seconds. Settling: "Now slowly let your hands come to rest in your lap… close your eyes… the warmth stays even after your hands move away… that warm feeling is your own kindness…" (30–35 seconds). Total ~65–80 seconds. Comfortable fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** This resolves the C-4 borderline question from the original inventory — with Kindness framing, it's a clean fit. Well-sourced. Excellent for the age range. Clear distinctiveness from other Kindness movement techniques.

### DOMAIN 5: RELAXATION / EMBODIED COPING (Bork — Firefly)

**Domain scope:** Progressive relaxation, body softening, mindful movement, letting go, rest. Movement techniques in this domain use the body to practice RELEASING — letting go of held tension, effort, and control.

---

#### RE-M1: Ragdoll Flop / Full-Body Release ("Melting")

**Clinical name:** Standing progressive release / Gravity-assisted muscle release
**Kid-friendly working name:** Melting

**Description:** The child stands tall, then slowly "melts" downward — first the head drops, then the shoulders fold, then the arms hang, then the whole upper body folds forward until they're dangling like a ragdoll. They hang there for 5–10 seconds, completely limp. Then they VERY slowly rebuild — stacking vertebra by vertebra back to standing. The settling portion is standing tall with eyes closed, noticing how the muscles feel after being completely released.

**Sources:**
- Kabat-Zinn (MBSR yoga — progressive relaxation through gravity-assisted release; "letting gravity have your body" as surrender instruction)
- Levine (the body's natural settling response involves release-from-top-down; the "melting" pattern mirrors how animals release after threat passes)
- van der Kolk (body-based relaxation approaches; gravity-assisted release bypasses cognitive resistance to relaxation)
- Kaiser Greenland (children's yoga — "melting candle" or "ragdoll" as named practice; progressive release in a playful frame)

**Domain assignment:** Relaxation / Embodied Coping (Bork). This is PURE relaxation through release — no effort, no attention training, no courage required. The body learns what "letting go" FEELS like, physically. The melting metaphor makes surrender appealing rather than scary. This is Bork's core territory: the body softening, tension leaving, rest arriving.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike M12 (body scan — systematic awareness of body parts while lying/sitting) and M13 (acceptance — mental permission to stop trying), this uses GRAVITY and MOVEMENT to achieve release. The body doesn't just notice tension — it lets gravity PULL the tension away. Different mechanism from M11 (PMR — tense then release) because there's no tensing phase: this is release-only.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. "Melt like a candle" is immediately understood. The slow rebuild (stacking up) is satisfying and provides coordination practice. No strength or flexibility issues — the child releases as far as THEIR body goes. The forward fold portion may cause brief head rush; the slow speed mitigates this.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (melt down ~15 seconds + hang ~10 seconds + rebuild ~15 seconds) takes ~40–50 seconds. Settling: "Now stand tall, eyes closed… feel how soft your body is… every muscle just got to rest for a moment… this is what letting go feels like…" (30–35 seconds). Total ~70–85 seconds. Good fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Well-supported across multiple sources. Playful, age-appropriate framing. Clean domain fit. Strong distinctiveness from existing relaxation techniques. The gravity-assisted mechanism is clinically interesting — it bypasses the "try to relax" paradox.

---

#### RE-M2: Gentle Stretching Sequence ("Sleepy Cat Stretch")

**Clinical name:** Mindful stretching / Somatic release through gentle extension
**Kid-friendly working name:** Sleepy Cat Stretch

**Description:** The child performs 3 simple stretches in sequence, holding each for 8–10 seconds: (1) Arms overhead stretch (reaching toward the ceiling), (2) Side bend (reaching one arm over, leaning gently to the side, then the other side), (3) Standing twist (arms out, gentle rotation left, then right). Each stretch is done slowly with attention to sensation. The settling portion has the child return to neutral standing, eyes closed, feeling the released, open quality in their body.

**Sources:**
- Kabat-Zinn (MBSR yoga — gentle stretching as mindful movement; emphasis on sensation awareness during stretch, not achieving a shape)
- van der Kolk (stretching releases fascia-held tension; gentle movement allows the body to discharge stored stress without reactivation)
- Kaiser Greenland (adapted yoga sequences for children — 2–3 poses maximum, emphasis on how it FEELS not how it LOOKS)
- Thich Nhat Hanh (gentle movement with awareness; "stretching is not about the stretch — it's about feeling alive")

**Domain assignment:** Relaxation / Embodied Coping (Bork). The stretches serve one purpose: releasing held tension so the body can rest. This isn't about attention training (Luna) or courage (Benson) — it's about giving the body permission to open, extend, and let go. The settling afterward IS the relaxation landing.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike RE-M1 (gravity-assisted release, folding downward), this is EXTENSION — reaching outward and upward. Different movement direction, different muscle groups, different sensation quality (stretch vs. release). Unlike M11 (PMR — tense then release specific muscles), this uses elongation, not contraction. The 3-stretch sequence teaches that relaxation can be ACTIVE — you don't have to lie still to let go.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. These are the three simplest stretches. Every child in this age range can reach overhead, lean to the side, and twist gently. No balance challenge (feet stay planted). No flexibility requirement (stretch to YOUR comfortable edge). The "sleepy cat" framing provides a delightful model — cats stretch before settling into rest.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (3 stretches × 8–10 seconds each, with transitions) takes ~40–50 seconds. Settling: "Now let everything go… stand easy… close your eyes… feel how your body says 'ahhhh' after a good stretch…" (30–35 seconds). Total ~70–85 seconds. Good fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Clinically validated as relaxation practice. Perfect age-appropriate simplicity. Clean domain fit. Clear distinctiveness from all existing Bork techniques. The 3-stretch format is simple enough for one-instruction-at-a-time delivery.

---

#### RE-M3: Tension Sigh / Squeeze-and-Release with Exhale ("Big Sigh")

**Clinical name:** Whole-body contraction with audible exhale release
**Kid-friendly working name:** Big Sigh

**Description:** The child squeezes their entire body tight — fists clenched, shoulders up to ears, face scrunched, whole body contracted into a ball of tension — while taking a deep breath in. Then they release EVERYTHING at once with a big, loud exhale sigh: "Ahhhhhh." The whole body goes limp as the breath leaves. Repeat 3–4 times. Each cycle: tighten + inhale → release + loud sigh. The settling portion extends the final release into stillness.

**Sources:**
- Levine (whole-body tension-release as stress cycle completion; the sigh is the body's natural "all clear" signal)
- Kabat-Zinn (body scan principle applied dynamically — noticing the contrast between tense and released states)
- Saltzman (breath-body practices for children; "tighten everything and let it all go" as age-appropriate relaxation)
- Shanker (whole-body discharge as biological regulation; the audible sigh gives the release a measurable endpoint)

**Domain assignment:** Relaxation / Embodied Coping (Bork). This is a whole-body version of M11's principle (Squeeze & Release), but with a key difference: M11 is Bramble-domain PROGRESSIVE muscle relaxation (systematic, body-part-by-body-part, interoceptive). RE-M3 is Bork-domain WHOLE-BODY release (everything at once, focused on the RELEASE and the SIGH, not on body-part discrimination). The intent is relaxation, not body awareness.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike M11 (progressive, sequential, body-part attention), this is SIMULTANEOUS (whole body, all at once). Unlike RE-M1 (gravity release, no tension phase), this has a deliberate tension phase. Unlike RE-M2 (stretching, extension), this is contraction→release. The SIGH adds an auditory release that no other technique uses — the child literally hears themselves let go. Note overlap risk with M11: if both are scheduled in the same arc, their distinctiveness must be reinforced through framing.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. "Squeeze your whole body tight… now let it ALL go" is instantly understood. The loud sigh is satisfying and fun — kids love being given permission to make noise. No balance, strength, or flexibility requirements. Can be done standing or seated.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (3–4 squeeze-sigh cycles) takes ~35–45 seconds. Settling: "One more time, let everything go… and this time, stay in the soft, melty feeling… close your eyes… notice how QUIET your body is after all that squeezing and sighing…" (30–40 seconds). Total ~65–85 seconds. Excellent fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Well-supported. High engagement factor (kids love the sigh). Clean domain fit with careful differentiation from M11. The audible exhale component is clinically meaningful — it makes the release tangible and memorable.

### DOMAIN 6: BODY AWARENESS / INTEROCEPTION (Bramble — Bear)

**Domain scope:** Noticing body signals. Locating sensations. Understanding what your body is telling you. Movement techniques in this domain use movement to create SENSATIONS worth noticing — the movement generates the signal, and the awareness skill is detecting it.

---

#### BI-M1: Stomping and Grounding ("Thunder Feet")

**Clinical name:** Percussive grounding / Proprioceptive foot awareness
**Kid-friendly working name:** Thunder Feet

**Description:** The child stamps their feet firmly on the floor — not jumping, just pressing each foot down hard, alternating left-right, like marching in place with emphasis. They feel the impact travel up through their feet, ankles, legs. After 15–20 seconds of deliberate stomping, they stop and stand still with eyes closed, noticing the tingling, buzzing, alive feeling in their feet and legs — the echo of the impact.

**Sources:**
- van der Kolk (grounding through proprioceptive impact; feet-on-ground contact as the most direct body-to-earth connection; proprioceptive input as regulatory anchor)
- Levine (grounding through the feet; "feel your feet on the floor" as the most common somatic experiencing directive; percussive impact amplifies the sensation)
- Kaiser Greenland (stomping games as children's grounding practice; children respond to percussive activities with high engagement)
- Kabat-Zinn (walking meditation — foot awareness; stomping is amplified foot awareness)

**Domain assignment:** Body Awareness / Interoception (Bramble). The stomping generates a SIGNAL — tingling, vibration, warmth in the feet and legs. The practice is in NOTICING that signal. "What does your body feel like after all that stomping? Where do you feel it? Is it in your feet? Your knees? Higher?" The child learns to read body signals that they normally ignore.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike M9 (5-senses grounding — external sensory awareness) and M11 (PMR — tense/release in specific muscle groups), this uses IMPACT to create sensation. The proprioceptive input from stomping is qualitatively different from tension (M11) or ambient sensory awareness (M9). The post-stomping tingling is a novel body signal that most children have never deliberately noticed.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Stomping is universally understood and deeply satisfying at ages 7–10. Concern: noise level. In a therapy office with thin walls or an apartment with downstairs neighbors, loud stomping may be impractical. DESIGN CHOICE: include a "quiet thunder" option — pressing feet firmly without lifting them, like pushing the floor away. Same proprioceptive input, less noise.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (15–20 seconds of stomping + brief "quiet thunder" option) takes ~25–35 seconds. Settling: "Now stand completely still… feel your feet, really feel them… they're tingling, aren't they? Buzzing, alive… that's your body talking to you, telling you 'I'm HERE, I'm RIGHT HERE on the ground'…" (30–40 seconds). Total ~55–75 seconds. Good fit with generous settling time.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Well-supported through proprioceptive grounding literature. High engagement. Clean domain fit. Strong distinctiveness. The "quiet thunder" alternative addresses the only feasibility concern (noise).

---

#### BI-M2: Body Squeeze Survey ("Bear Hug Scan")

**Clinical name:** Sequential body awareness through self-compression
**Kid-friendly working name:** Bear Hug Scan

**Description:** The child "squeezes" different body parts in sequence, one at a time, and notices the sensation: squeeze both fists (5 seconds, release, notice), then squeeze both arms against torso (5 seconds, release, notice), then scrunch shoulders to ears (5 seconds, release, notice), then squeeze face tight (5 seconds, release, notice). Each squeeze-release cycle is followed by a moment of NOTICING: "What does that body part feel like now?" The practice is a body scan using compression as the awareness amplifier.

**Sources:**
- Levine (sequential body engagement as interoceptive training; the squeeze creates sensation that the child then learns to detect and locate)
- van der Kolk (body-based awareness approaches; systematic body attention through active engagement)
- Kabat-Zinn (body scan principle — systematic attention to body parts; this version uses compression to make each part "louder" and easier to notice)
- Saltzman (body awareness practices for children; sequential body engagement as age-appropriate body scan alternative)

**Domain assignment:** Body Awareness / Interoception (Bramble). This IS a body scan — but instead of lying still and trying to notice subtle sensations (M12), the child CREATES obvious sensations by squeezing each body part. This is Bramble's territory: learning to notice what your body is telling you. The squeeze is the amplifier. The noticing is the skill.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike M11 (PMR — tense/release focused on RELAXATION), this is focused on DETECTION. The goal isn't to relax the muscles; the goal is to NOTICE them. The question after each squeeze isn't "feel how soft it is now" (Bork territory) but "can you feel it? Is it warm? Tingly? Heavy? That's your body talking." Unlike BI-M1 (feet-focused proprioceptive impact), this surveys the WHOLE body part by part.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Squeezing body parts is simple and engaging. The sequential pattern (hands → arms → shoulders → face) is easy to follow. No balance, strength, or flexibility requirements. The "bear hug" framing matches Bramble perfectly.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (4 body parts × ~8 seconds each including squeeze + notice) takes ~35–45 seconds. Settling: "Now relax everything… close your eyes… see if you can feel ALL those body parts at once… your hands, your arms, your shoulders, your face… they're all talking to you, all the time — now you know how to listen…" (30–35 seconds). Total ~65–80 seconds. Good fit.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Well-supported. Clear distinction from M11 (relaxation intent) and M12 (passive scanning). Clean Bramble domain fit. The self-compression amplification principle is clinically sound — it bridges the gap between body scans that work for adults (who can detect subtle sensation) and what children need (amplified, obvious sensation to build the awareness skill).

---

#### BI-M3: Single-Foot Press / Weight Awareness ("Earth Feels")

**Clinical name:** Unilateral weight-bearing awareness / Proprioceptive weight transfer
**Kid-friendly working name:** Earth Feels

**Description:** The child stands normally, then slowly shifts ALL their weight onto one foot. They notice what changes: the foot gets heavier, the ankle works harder, the other foot gets lighter. They hold the weighted side for 8–10 seconds, noticing everything. Then slowly shift to the other foot. The practice teaches interoceptive detection of WEIGHT — a sensation that's always present but rarely noticed. The settling is standing on both feet, eyes closed, noticing the balanced weight.

**Sources:**
- Kabat-Zinn (MBSR walking meditation — weight shifting as awareness practice; "notice the moment when one foot bears your full weight")
- Thich Nhat Hanh (walking meditation — "feel the earth supporting you through your feet")
- Levine (grounding through the felt sense of weight and gravity; the body's relationship to the earth as foundational regulation)
- Kaiser Greenland (weight-shifting games as children's balance/awareness training)

**Domain assignment:** Body Awareness / Interoception (Bramble). The practice is pure interoception — detecting the sensation of WEIGHT in different body parts. "Can you feel the difference between your heavy foot and your light foot?" This is body-signal literacy: learning to read what your body is doing at this moment.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike BI-M1 (stomping — creating sensation through impact), this notices EXISTING sensation (weight) through contrast. Unlike BI-M2 (squeeze survey — whole body systematic compression), this focuses on a single axis: weight distribution. Unlike PM-M3 (balance pose — attention training through challenge), this stays well within normal balance range — both feet touching the floor at all times, just with weight shifted. The intent is NOTICING, not CHALLENGING.

**Age appropriateness:** Excellent. Weight shifting requires zero athletic ability. Both feet stay on the ground at all times (no balance risk). The sensation is immediately detectable — every child can feel the difference between a heavy foot and a light foot. Simple and quiet. Works in any space.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (two weight shifts, 8–10 seconds each, with transition) takes ~25–35 seconds. Settling: "Now find the middle… both feet sharing the weight… eyes closed… notice how your body knows EXACTLY how to balance the weight, all by itself, without you telling it to… that's your body being smart…" (30–40 seconds). Total ~55–75 seconds. Excellent fit with generous settling time.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Well-supported through walking meditation literature applied to weight-awareness specifically. Extremely simple and feasible. Clean domain fit. Clear distinctiveness from all other movement techniques in this inventory.

---

#### BI-M4: Heartbeat Hunt After Movement ("Find Your Drum")

**Clinical name:** Post-exertion interoceptive awareness / Heartbeat detection through exercise priming
**Kid-friendly working name:** Find Your Drum

**Description:** The child does 15–20 seconds of vigorous movement (jumping jacks, running in place, or fast arm circles) to get their heart rate up. Then they stop immediately, place one hand on their chest, close their eyes, and TRY TO FEEL THEIR HEARTBEAT. The exercise makes the heartbeat louder and easier to detect. They hold still for 20–30 seconds, tracking the heartbeat as it gradually slows. The practice teaches heartbeat detection — the most fundamental interoceptive signal.

**Sources:**
- Porges (Polyvagal Theory — interoception as a regulation pathway; heartbeat awareness is the foundational interoceptive skill; children who can detect their heartbeat show better emotional regulation)
- van der Kolk (reconnecting with body signals; heartbeat as the most direct connection to the body's arousal state)
- Kaiser Greenland (heartbeat exercises for children — exercise-then-listen format; children find heartbeat detection fascinating when it's made detectable)
- Levine (felt sense through internal body awareness; the heartbeat is the body's most constant signal)

**Domain assignment:** Body Awareness / Interoception (Bramble). This is PURE interoception — detecting an internal body signal. The exercise portion is just a setup to make the heartbeat detectable. The real practice is the listening — "Can you feel it? Can you count the beats? Notice them getting slower?" This teaches the child that their body has an internal rhythm they can learn to detect and interpret.

**Distinctiveness:** Unlike BI-M1 (external impact, feet focus), BI-M2 (compression survey, whole body), and BI-M3 (weight awareness, gravity), this targets an INTERNAL ORGAN signal. The heartbeat is qualitatively different from muscular or proprioceptive sensation — it's autonomous, rhythmic, and emotionally informative (heartbeat changes with emotions). This is the deepest interoceptive practice in the inventory.

**Age appropriateness:** Good with important design notes. The vigorous movement portion must be brief (15–20 seconds) — this is NOT an exercise module, it's a heart-detection priming step. Some children may struggle to find their heartbeat even after exercise. DESIGN CHOICE: "If you can't feel it, that's OK — just notice your chest moving up and down, or the warm feeling in your body. Your heart is always beating, even when you can't feel it." The hand-on-chest positioning helps. This connects to G-5 (Heartbeat Listening / Interoception) from the original inventory, which was rated STRONG as a seated technique. This is the movement-enhanced version.

**Phase B feasibility:** Movement portion (15–20 seconds of vigorous movement + transition to stillness) takes ~20–25 seconds. Heartbeat detection + settling: "Hand on your chest… be very very still… can you feel it? That bump bump bump? That's YOUR drum, beating just for you… now notice — it's getting slower, quieter… your body is settling all by itself…" (40–50 seconds). Total ~60–75 seconds. Good fit. The generous settling time is clinically appropriate here — the heart rate recovery IS the practice.

**Strength rating: STRONG.** Porges explicitly identifies heartbeat interoception as foundational for emotional regulation. The exercise-priming approach is specifically recommended for children by Kaiser Greenland. Clean domain fit. Extremely high distinctiveness — no other technique in the entire inventory uses heartbeat detection.
